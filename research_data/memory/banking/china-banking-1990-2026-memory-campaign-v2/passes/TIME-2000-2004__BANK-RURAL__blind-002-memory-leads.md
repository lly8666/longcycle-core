## Orthogonal falsification question

Do the early-2000s legal-form experiments, province-level coordination, and public recapitalization create multiple genuinely separate period-wide BANK-RURAL models, or are they alternative governance/legal mechanisms within a common RCC-lineage reform regime?

## A. Legal form versus economic lineage

1. Unified county legal-person RCC, rural cooperative-bank and rural commercial-bank forms can all arise from the same RCC predecessor lineage.
2. A legal conversion changes governance and capital rules but does not by itself erase inherited borrowers, branches, staff, bad assets or local political relationships.
3. Therefore legal form and economic lineage must be tracked separately.
4. “农村商业银行” should not automatically be interpreted as economically equivalent to a city commercial bank; RCC origin and county franchise remain decisive boundary signals.
5. “农村合作银行” can represent an intermediate governance form rather than a separate long-run business model.
6. Unified legal-person reform can centralize a county system without converting it to a commercial-bank form.
7. A county can therefore experience material governance change while preserving the RCC name.
8. Prosperous counties are more likely to support conventional shareholding/commercial forms because local enterprises and households can supply capital.
9. Poorer counties may remain RCC structures longer because weak capital formation constrains legal-form conversion.
10. This heterogeneity reflects local economic capacity, not necessarily distinct policy objectives.
11. Legal-form choice may be endogenous to pre-reform asset quality and franchise strength, creating selection bias when comparing outcomes.
12. Early successful RCBs should not be used as representative evidence for all RCC reform.
13. A post-conversion rise in commercial/SME lending may reflect the county economy rather than the legal form itself.
14. The strongest ontology remains one RCC-lineage family with multiple reform endpoints.

## B. Provincial-union control versus county autonomy

15. Provincial rural credit unions are better understood as a service/management/control layer than as ordinary deposit-taking parent banks.
16. Their functions can include clearing, IT, training, risk guidance, personnel influence and system coordination.
17. Personnel appointment power is economically more consequential than a simple “service platform” description.
18. County boards can be legally responsible while provincial unions exercise strong practical management influence.
19. This creates a hybrid governance structure: decentralized legal persons plus centralized administrative/service authority.
20. Hybrid control can improve standardization while weakening board accountability because authority and liability are not perfectly aligned.
21. Provincial coordination can reduce duplicated technology cost and improve small-bank access to payments infrastructure.
22. It can also create implicit expectations that stronger institutions support weaker ones or that the provincial system will prevent failures.
23. Such implicit support may weaken market discipline without producing formal consolidated-group liability.
24. Province-wide technology and clearing dependence can generate operational concentration risk.
25. The provincial-union layer may therefore resemble a quasi-group in some economic dimensions, but it is not itself a second period-wide rural-bank balance-sheet model.
26. Province-to-province differences in union authority are important later Evidence questions.
27. A county institution’s legal independence should never be inferred solely from separate financial statements if management authority is centralized elsewhere.
28. Conversely, strong provincial control should not justify mechanically consolidating all county balance sheets.

## C. Public recapitalization as loss allocation and incentive contract

29. Central-bank/public support should be analyzed as restructuring capital/loss absorption, not ordinary wholesale funding.
30. Special support instruments can remove or neutralize historical-loss burdens while requiring local capital/governance actions.
31. Conditionality is economically central: public resources are intended to purchase reform behavior as well as solvency improvement.
32. Exact thresholds, redemption criteria and accounting treatment are Evidence-stage and should not be invented.
33. A support instrument may create a receivable/asset-side accounting effect rather than immediate cash capital; instrument mechanics matter.
34. Historical NPL removal and fresh capital are separate components even when bundled in one reform package.
35. Local-government contributions can take multiple forms and may not be economically equivalent to cash equity.
36. Recovery of old bad assets after support can create later upside whose ownership/accounting treatment should be checked.
37. Public support may induce moral hazard if stakeholders expect future rescues.
38. But withholding support can leave a viable rural deposit franchise trapped beneath unserviceable legacy losses.
39. Reform success should therefore be judged on post-cleanup new-vintage asset quality, governance and earnings, not only the opening NPL ratio.
40. A sharp NPL-ratio decline after restructuring can occur through asset transfer/write-off and denominator growth without equivalent cash recovery.
41. Capital-ratio improvement can likewise reflect accounting perimeter, new shares and support instruments.
42. Public recapitalization is a cross-form reform mechanism applying to RCCs and converted institutions rather than a separate business model.

## D. Shareholding/member capital and governance after conversion

43. Converting cooperative member shares into more permanent/conventional capital can strengthen loss absorption, but actual governance depends on shareholder concentration.
44. Local enterprises can become meaningful shareholders and simultaneously major borrowers, creating related-party risk.
45. Employee/manager participation can improve incentives or entrench insiders depending on board/control architecture.
46. Local government-linked capital can stabilize institutions while preserving political influence over credit allocation.
47. Dispersed farmer/member shares may provide weak effective oversight even when legally important.
48. Qualification/member-share versus investment-share distinctions are likely material to voting/withdrawal/capital permanence; exact definitions require Evidence.
49. A rural commercial-bank label does not guarantee dispersed private ownership or independent governance.
50. A rural cooperative-bank label does not guarantee genuine member control.
51. Ownership reform should therefore be tested against board appointment, connected lending, management incentives and capital permanence.
52. Legal ownership “clarification” is a necessary but insufficient condition for commercial discipline.
53. Shareholder concentration may be more predictive of governance failure than formal cooperative-versus-commercial label.

## E. Rural microcredit, joint guarantees and franchise changes

54. Household microcredit and joint-guarantee products represent process innovation inside the rural franchise rather than a separate bank model.
55. Credit-household/village rating mechanisms attempt to formalize soft local information.
56. Joint guarantees substitute social collateral for physical collateral but can transmit correlated shocks across borrowers.
57. Small-ticket standardization can improve scale but may weaken relationship judgment if targets dominate underwriting.
58. County RCB/RCB-like institutions in industrial regions can become strong SME lenders while retaining rural lineage.
59. State-bank county/rural branch retrenchment can increase RCC market share without improving borrower quality.
60. Market-share gains can increase policy pressure because RCCs become the residual formal lender in weak counties.
61. Deposit concentration in RCCs after competitor retreat can improve funding but increase local systemic importance.
62. Franchise economics therefore depend on local competitor structure, not only reform legal form.
63. Household lending, agriculture lending and SME lending should remain separate portfolio categories even if all support the “三农” economy.
64. Stronger eastern counties may diversify away from agriculture earlier; weaker counties may remain policy/service intensive.
65. This is regional franchise heterogeneity inside the same institution lineage.

## F. NPL, capital and perimeter semantics after restructuring

66. Legal-person consolidation creates a structural break in institution counts and financial reporting.
67. Multiple grassroots RCC entities can disappear statistically while their assets/liabilities survive inside a county legal person.
68. Comparing pre- and post-unification averages per institution is therefore misleading.
69. Provincial-system aggregates can mix independent county legal persons and provincial service entities.
70. NPL ratios after support must distinguish write-off, transfer, reclassification, collection and denominator expansion.
71. Historical-loss stock should be tagged separately from new-vintage NPL formation.
72. Capital ratios before and after conversion may use different capital definitions/perimeters.
73. Member-share capital and conventional bank equity should not be assumed economically identical.
74. Support-instrument accounting can distort naïve comparisons of assets, capital or earnings.
75. Credit-station closure/formalization creates outlet-count breaks; fewer legal outlets need not mean less service reach.
76. Core-system centralization improves data quality and may itself increase recognized problem loans through better visibility.
77. Reported profitability can rise after cleanup because legacy provisioning/interest-recognition burdens change, not only because franchise economics improve.
78. Post-restructuring ROA/ROE comparisons need opening-balance-sheet normalization.
79. Province-union service fees/technology costs may shift expenses between county institutions and provincial layers.

## G. Failure-mode and counterexample challenge

80. A county can receive strong public recapitalization yet regenerate bad loans if local-government or insider credit capture persists.
81. A province can centralize IT and personnel yet fail to create accountable boards.
82. A commercial-bank conversion can improve capital while worsening shareholder-related lending.
83. A cooperative-bank form can outperform a commercial-bank form if local governance and franchise are stronger.
84. A unified RCC can remain financially healthy without converting if its local economy, capital and management are sound.
85. A weak agricultural county can remain structurally low-return despite successful balance-sheet cleanup because operating costs and credit demand are unfavorable.
86. A prosperous manufacturing county can show strong earnings but carry concentrated SME/guarantee-chain tail risk.
87. A county with major postal-savings outflow can remain funding constrained despite institutional reform.
88. A county gaining deposits from state-bank branch withdrawal can simultaneously inherit weaker borrowers and public-service obligations.
89. These counterexamples reject legal-form determinism and support a common framework of lineage + governance + franchise + loss allocation.

## H. Contemporaneous expectations likely to be falsified later

90. Expectation: once historical losses are removed, rural banks become healthy. Counterlead: new-vintage underwriting and governance determine persistence.
91. Expectation: provincial unions solve fragmented governance. Counterlead: they can create authority/liability mismatch and administrative control.
92. Expectation: RCB conversion creates commercial discipline. Counterlead: ownership concentration and local political influence can persist.
93. Expectation: cooperative-bank conversion restores member governance. Counterlead: nominal cooperative ownership may remain weak.
94. Expectation: stronger capital ratios prove economic recovery. Counterlead: support, accounting and denominator effects can dominate early metrics.
95. Expectation: household microcredit solves rural access. Counterlead: small loans remain costly and correlated shocks can generate widespread stress.
96. Expectation: state-bank rural retreat benefits RCCs. Counterlead: it can turn them into residual lenders with heavier policy burdens.
97. Expectation: centralized core systems eliminate operational risk. Counterlead: technology centralization changes the risk locus rather than eliminating fraud/governance failures.

## I. Boundary checks

98. Rural commercial banks remain BANK-RURAL when their institutional lineage is RCC-based, even when customers are urbanizing or SME-heavy.
99. City commercial banks remain BANK-CITY even if they operate county branches or serve rural borrowers.
100. ABC stays BANK-STATE-OWNED; its rural branch withdrawal/lending is environment/bridge context only.
101. Policy-bank agriculture lending remains BANK-POLICY-DEVELOPMENT.
102. Postal savings is an external funds-flow institution in this slice and should not be merged into RCC balance sheets.
103. Village banks should not be projected backward into 2000–2004.
104. Provincial unions should not automatically be consolidated as a single provincial bank.

## Orthogonal disposition

No second period-wide BANK-RURAL structural category emerged. Unified RCCs, rural cooperative banks and rural commercial banks are best modeled as alternative legal/governance outcomes within the RCC lineage; provincial unions are a cross-entity control/service layer; public recapitalization is a historical-loss allocation and incentive mechanism. Regional franchise, ownership concentration and governance quality explain more than legal form alone. The 2000–2004 slice can therefore be marked broad+orthogonal complete and the next eligible invocation should advance to 2005–2008 broad recall.
