## Period frame

- 2020-2022 combines an acute pandemic shock, policy-directed credit support, loan/payment relief, rapid digital operating changes, lower loan pricing, property-sector deterioration, mortgage confidence stress, continued LGFV/infrastructure support, wealth-management net-value transition and growing NIM pressure.
- The large state-owned banks were simultaneously commercial lenders, policy-transmission channels, major mortgage banks, wealth distributors, systemic liquidity anchors and digital-payment participants; the same policy can have different effects across these roles.
- A central analytical problem is delayed recognition: temporary relief or restructuring can genuinely bridge cash-flow disruption, but it can also postpone the moment at which persistent borrower weakness becomes NPL/ECL loss.

## Pandemic credit support and forbearance

1. Loan-payment deferrals and principal/interest relief should be separated from contractual restructuring, NPL classification and ECL staging; they are not automatically the same event.
2. A borrower that missed revenue during a lockdown but recovered after reopening is a genuine-forbearance-success counterexample to the assumption that all relief hid losses.
3. Repeated extensions for structurally weak borrowers can become evergreening; frequency and post-relief cash flow matter more than the existence of relief alone.
4. Policy pressure to lend to SMEs, manufacturers, health-related firms and infrastructure likely lowered some loan spreads while raising required origination capacity.
5. Large banks could use digital channels and existing payroll/supply-chain data to extend support without full branch access.
6. Pandemic lending can improve near-term reported asset quality through government-linked borrowers and denominator growth even while older retail/SME cohorts deteriorate.
7. Emergency working-capital loans should be separated from long-term investment credit; repayment depends on reopening cash flow rather than project completion.
8. Policy-supported loan growth can be commercially attractive if funding costs fall and guarantee/collateral quality is strong; it is not necessarily loss-making directed credit.
9. The quality of pandemic-era credit likely varied sharply by borrower: central SOEs, local SOEs, exporters, restaurants, tourism, transport, manufacturers and digital firms experienced different shocks.
10. Regional lockdown intensity created geographic credit heterogeneity inside the same bank, making national averages less informative.

## ECL, NPL, overlays and recognition

11. ECL models calibrated on normal history may have required management overlays during the pandemic because forward-looking macro relationships broke down.
12. A management overlay can raise provisions before realized defaults and later be released; provision movement is therefore partly judgment, not only mechanical realized loss.
13. Stage 2 exposure may capture borrowers receiving relief or showing increased credit risk before Stage 3/NPL status.
14. Relief programs can complicate the interpretation of days past due because contractual schedules themselves may change.
15. NPL stock, NPL formation, Stage 2 migration, overdue loans, write-offs and credit cost can move in different directions during a shock-and-relief period.
16. High provision coverage can coexist with latent risk if the NPL denominator is restrained by relief/restructuring; allowance-to-loan and Stage 2 data may provide different signals.
17. A later NPL increase may reflect recognition of problems originating during 2020 rather than new 2022 underwriting failure.
18. Conversely, an early high credit-cost year followed by releases can indicate front-loaded provisioning rather than worsening later credit.
19. Mortgage and developer exposures should not be pooled in asset-quality analysis; household mortgage losses can remain low while developers default.
20. Write-off/disposal intensity matters for interpreting stable NPL ratios during property and SME stress.

## LPR, pricing, deposits and NIM

21. LPR-linked loan repricing made policy-rate reductions more visible in asset yields, but repricing timing differs between new loans and back-book loans.
22. Loan repricing can be faster than deposit repricing, creating margin compression even for deposit-rich state banks.
23. Pandemic precautionary savings may have temporarily boosted household deposits, potentially lowering marginal funding stress despite falling asset yields.
24. Deposit growth can reflect reduced consumption/investment opportunities rather than improved franchise strength.
25. Corporate deposit behavior may differ: cash-rich SOEs can accumulate deposits while stressed SMEs draw them down.
26. Time-deposit migration or longer maturities can raise average funding cost even when total deposits grow.
27. Structural-deposit regulation/normalization may push banks toward plain time deposits, WMPs or other funding substitutes; exact timing and rules require Evidence.
28. NIM decline versus net-interest-income growth should be separated because large asset growth can offset lower margins.
29. Lower lending rates can be both policy-driven and competitive; borrower mix and risk-adjusted pricing matter.
30. Internal FTP becomes more important when head office wants branches to support low-priced policy sectors without destroying local profitability signals.
31. Mortgage repricing, corporate LPR-linked repricing and inclusive-loan pricing can have different lags and margin impacts.
32. Fee income can be affected by reduced branch activity, payment-fee concessions, wealth-market volatility, cards and cross-border travel/trade changes.

## Property developers, mortgages and presale/project risk

33. Developer credit risk accelerated as highly levered property firms lost refinancing access and sales slowed; bank exposure can include loans, bonds, guarantees, wealth assets and supply-chain borrowers.
34. Direct developer-loan balances do not capture construction contractors, materials suppliers, trust/WMP holdings or mortgages on unfinished projects.
35. Presale/project risk introduces a completion channel: a household may be willing and able to pay but resist payment if the home is not delivered.
36. 2022 mortgage-payment refusal/boycott episodes are a high-value memory lead; exact cities, balances and bank exposures require later sourcing.
37. "保交楼" / ensuring project completion is a useful period-native search key, but bank-specific roles and policy timing remain unverified here.
38. Mortgage credit risk should distinguish completed-home mortgages, presale mortgages, investment properties and first-home owner-occupiers.
39. CCB is likely the strongest heterogeneity check for mortgage/housing concentration because of its historical housing franchise.
40. Large state banks may have had better geographic diversification than regional banks, reducing single-city property concentration while preserving national-cycle exposure.
41. Developer distress can hurt collateral values and household confidence before mortgage defaults rise.
42. Banks can support viable project completion while refusing new unsecured funding to a distressed developer group; project-level and parent-level credit decisions differ.
43. M&A financing or project takeover by stronger developers/SOEs can reduce completion risk without curing all legacy creditor losses.
44. Property loan concentration caps or related rules matter here only through portfolio steering; exact regulatory thresholds belong to the sealed system shard/Evidence later.
45. A counterexample is that high-quality mortgages in stronger cities can remain resilient despite severe developer bond defaults.

## LGFV, local government and infrastructure

46. Pandemic and growth-stabilization infrastructure support likely increased demand for state-bank credit to transport, utilities, municipal projects and related SOEs.
47. LGFV risk should distinguish explicit local-government bonds, platform loans, project loans, operating SOE credit and land-linked repayment sources.
48. Property weakness can indirectly weaken LGFV repayment through land-sale/fiscal channels even when an LGFV has no direct developer exposure.
49. Strong provinces/cities can remain low-risk counterexamples while weaker jurisdictions face refinancing pressure.
50. State banks can prefer government-bond holdings over opaque platform loans, changing yield, duration and capital usage without eliminating local-fiscal concentration.
51. Infrastructure lending can be policy-supportive and commercially sound where project cash flows or fiscal support are strong.
52. Longer maturities and refinancing can reduce near-term default risk while increasing duration and long-run return pressure.

## Retail, cards, consumer and SME cycles

53. Card and unsecured consumer credit likely deteriorated earlier than mortgages for workers/business owners hit by lockdowns and income loss.
54. Revolving-card balances, installment loans and general consumer loans have different loss timing and customer behavior.
55. Travel and offline-consumption suppression can reduce card fee income even while credit balances fall, creating both risk and revenue effects.
56. Digital consumer lending may sustain origination during branch closures but can select into riskier or more financially stressed customers if growth targets dominate.
57. Small-business loan quality should be cohort-based: 2020 relief vintages, reopening vintages and later lockdown vintages can differ sharply.
58. Supply-chain finance anchored on strong SOEs may perform differently from unsecured micro loans despite both being reported as SME support.
59. Government guarantees or risk-sharing arrangements, where used, change loss allocation and pricing but should not be assumed universal.
60. Inclusive-finance metrics may grow rapidly while reported NPLs lag because new vintages are young.

## Wealth management and customer assets

61. Net-value transformation means WMP customers increasingly see mark-to-market gains/losses rather than stable quasi-deposit returns.
62. Customer loss tolerance and redemption behavior become liquidity-management variables for bank wealth subsidiaries/distributors.
63. 2022 bond-market volatility/redemption pressure is a possible period lead for WMP net-value products; exact episodes and bank-specific effects need Evidence.
64. A bank can face reputational and customer-retention losses from WMP drawdowns without direct balance-sheet credit losses.
65. Wealth-management subsidiaries, where operating, separate governance and capital boundaries but remain linked to parent-bank distribution channels and brand.
66. Deposit-to-WMP migration can reverse during market volatility, temporarily improving bank deposits while hurting wealth AUM and fees.
67. Custody assets and WMP AUM should not be counted as bank balance-sheet assets.
68. Legacy nonstandard assets can create transition-management challenges as products move toward standardized/net-value structures.

## Digital operations, payments and bank-specific incidents

69. Pandemic branch restrictions accelerated remote account service, mobile banking, video/online advisory, digital loan applications and centralized operations.
70. Operational resilience became more visible: remote work, data-center reliability, cyber defense, authentication and fraud monitoring were essential to continuity.
71. Digital adoption can permanently reduce low-value branch transactions while leaving branches important for complex wealth, corporate and elderly-customer service.
72. Digital RMB pilots are a state-bank business-model lead because large state banks acted as important wallet/service participants; system-policy design itself belongs elsewhere.
73. Platform payments continued to own customer interfaces, pushing banks to compete through app ecosystems, merchant services and embedded finance.
74. Fraud and mule-account controls can create tension with frictionless digital onboarding.
75. Bank of China's "原油宝" retail commodity-linked product episode in 2020 is a high-value failed-product/conduct-risk lead: extreme market pricing exposed product design, suitability, margin/liquidation, operational and customer-dispute risks. Exact contractual mechanics and losses require Evidence.
76. The 原油宝 episode is a counterexample to treating large state-bank retail risk as only credit risk; market, product, operational, conduct and reputational risks can dominate.
77. Digital customer growth should be distinguished from profitable active use; pandemic necessity can inflate registrations without equivalent monetization.

## Capital, TLAC and balance-sheet structure

78. Strong loan growth, lower margins and higher provisions can pressure internal capital generation even when headline capital ratios remain stable.
79. Retained earnings, RWA mix, capital instruments and slower high-risk-asset growth can offset pressure.
80. Large systemic banks faced growing loss-absorbing-capacity/TLAC considerations around this era; exact requirements, institutions and dates require Evidence and should not be asserted here.
81. Government and policy-related bond growth can reduce average RWA intensity relative to corporate lending while increasing duration exposure.
82. ECL allowance and regulatory capital interact but are not interchangeable; accounting provisions can affect earnings and capital through multiple channels.
83. Dividend policy is another capital-management channel for state-owned listed banks, with tension between shareholder cash returns and capital retention.

## Green, manufacturing and policy-favored credit

84. Green finance, advanced manufacturing, technology innovation and inclusive finance became increasingly important portfolio-growth categories for large state banks.
85. "Green" credit should be separated from project credit quality; policy classification does not guarantee commercial success.
86. Manufacturing-medium/long-term loans can support industrial upgrading but extend duration and expose banks to technology/industry-cycle risk.
87. Carbon-transition risk can affect traditional energy borrowers even while banks grow renewable financing.
88. Policy-favored sectors may receive lower pricing, increasing the need for funding-cost and capital efficiency to preserve returns.

## Contemporaneous judgment leads

103. Early-pandemic bull case: large state banks' deposit franchises, sovereign linkage and digital infrastructure would let them gain share while supporting the economy.
104. Early-pandemic bear case: relief policies would hide SME/retail losses and create a delayed NPL wave once support expired.
105. Property-era bull case: large banks had conservative mortgage underwriting, diversified regions and manageable direct developer exposure.
106. Property-era bear case: direct developer loans understated ecosystem exposure and unfinished projects could transmit from developers to mortgage borrowers and local finances.
107. Margin bear case: repeated LPR/loan repricing plus deposit stickiness would structurally compress NIM.
108. Counterview: strong deposit inflows, scale, digital efficiency and asset growth could offset part of margin pressure.
109. Wealth bull case: net-value transformation would create cleaner, more sustainable asset-management businesses.
110. Wealth bear case: visible mark-to-market losses would test customers accustomed to quasi-deposit stability and could drive redemptions back to deposits.

## Counterexamples / negative space

111. Forbearance is not necessarily evergreening; temporary shock borrowers can cure fully.
112. High pandemic loan growth is not necessarily lower credit quality if concentrated in strong SOEs, infrastructure or guaranteed borrowers.
113. Developer default does not automatically imply mortgage default; borrower willingness/ability and project completion are separate.
114. Mortgage-payment refusal can be driven by non-delivery rather than household insolvency.
115. Property-sector stress can increase deposits at the strongest banks through risk aversion even as their asset yields fall.
116. Lower NIM can coexist with higher profit if volume, fees or credit costs improve; conversely higher net interest income can coexist with weaker margin economics.
117. WMP redemption can help parent-bank deposit funding while hurting wealth fees/AUM.
118. Higher provisions can reflect prudent overlays rather than realized deterioration.
119. Digital migration can reduce branch transaction cost while increasing cybersecurity, fraud and technology spend.
120. Policy credit can be both stabilizing and commercially rational; the policy/commercial distinction is not binary.
