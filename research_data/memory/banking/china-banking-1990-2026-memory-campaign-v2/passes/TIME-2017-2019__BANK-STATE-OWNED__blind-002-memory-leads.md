## Orthogonal challenge: role decomposition before labels

1. A state bank's relationship to a WMP should be decomposed into product manager, distributor, custodian, proprietary investor, liquidity provider and reputational backstop; these roles can coexist but should not be collapsed into one exposure number.
2. Moving toward net-asset-value WMPs changes valuation and customer-loss allocation even if underlying credit assets are initially similar.
3. "Breaking implicit guarantees" is partly behavioral: customers may continue to expect support after legal/economic product boundaries change.
4. A bank can reduce direct nonstandard assets while still earning fees from origination, distribution or custody of similar financing elsewhere.
5. Wealth subsidiaries/asset-management subsidiaries, where established in this era, may have shifted governance and accounting boundaries before customer expectations fully changed; exact bank-by-bank dates require Evidence later.
6. Clean separation of proprietary and customer assets can lower balance-sheet opacity without automatically reducing economy-wide credit risk.
7. Product maturity mismatch, liquidity mismatch and credit impairment are distinct WMP risk mechanisms.
8. A bank's reputational decision to support a customer product is contingent and path-dependent, not equivalent to a contractual guarantee.

## Interbank and small-bank stress: state-bank roles

9. Deposit-rich state banks could be net lenders to the interbank market while also reducing unsecured limits to weak counterparties.
10. System liquidity can be ample while institution-specific funding remains scarce because credit segmentation blocks transmission.
11. A rise in secured repo versus unsecured lending would shift the key risk from pure counterparty exposure toward collateral quality, haircuts and eligible-asset availability.
12. Large banks can transmit stress by changing haircuts, maturities or counterparty limits even if they do not withdraw all funding.
13. Holdings of small-bank financial bonds or negotiable CDs are economically different from unsecured interbank placements and repo.
14. Policy requests for large banks to support smaller institutions may create a tension between systemic-stability roles and standalone risk-adjusted-return targets.
15. State banks can gain deposits during confidence episodes, increasing their liquidity-provider capacity while also concentrating system funding.
16. Interbank stress does not imply weak state-bank solvency; it can instead reveal the franchise value of broad retail/corporate deposits.
17. A counterexample to "deleveraging hurts large banks" is that tighter wholesale funding can improve their relative competitive position versus smaller peers.

## Private enterprise, bonds and credit allocation

18. Private-enterprise stress should be split into access-to-credit, pricing, collateral, guarantee-chain contagion, bond-market refinancing and borrower profitability.
19. A state bank may increase new private-enterprise lending while legacy private borrowers continue to default, so stock exposure and marginal allocation can move in opposite directions.
20. Policy encouragement for private firms can shift approval rules, branch quotas, collateral tolerance and pricing rather than simply increase aggregate volume.
21. Bond defaults provide market signals earlier or differently than bank classifications; bank risk systems may use bond prices, missed coupons or refinancing difficulty as early-warning variables.
22. State banks can be bond investors, underwriters, custodians and lenders to the same issuer; loss and conflict channels differ by role.
23. SOE support can crowd out private credit in some settings, but supply-side cleanup of weak SOEs can also release capacity and credit for stronger private firms.
24. Guarantee-chain unwinding is a useful failure mechanism because credit losses can propagate through contingent obligations without shared ownership.
25. Debt committees and coordinated restructurings can reduce disorderly defaults while delaying recognition of ultimate loss.
26. A restructuring that restores cash flow is a genuine-performing counterexample; not every maturity extension is evergreening.

## IFRS 9 / ECL versus regulatory asset quality

27. ECL staging and regulatory NPL classification are overlapping but non-identical lenses; Stage 2 deterioration can rise before Stage 3/NPL recognition.
28. Stage migration is a flow signal; the closing Stage 2 or Stage 3 balance is a stock.
29. Lifetime ECL for deteriorated assets can raise provisions before default, so higher credit cost need not mean same-period NPL formation.
30. Write-offs reduce reported NPL stock but do not reverse historical credit loss; recoveries after write-off are a separate outcome.
31. An NPL ratio can stabilize while Stage 2 exposure rises, creating a leading-risk signal invisible in the headline NPL ratio.
32. Conversely, Stage 2 can fall because accounts cure or migrate to Stage 3; direction alone is ambiguous.
33. Overdue-90-days, NPL classification and Stage 3 should not be assumed one-to-one without exact bank policy and regulatory timing.
34. Provision coverage uses NPL as denominator, whereas ECL allowance covers a wider asset set; cross-period comparisons need semantic caution.
35. Credit cost depends on average loans/credit exposure definitions and P&L charges, not only ending allowance.
36. Large write-off volumes may indicate accelerated cleanup rather than worsening new-vintage quality.
37. A useful cohort question is whether new inclusive/mortgage/corporate vintages perform better than 2013-2016 problem cohorts; aggregate NPLs mix them.

## Funding defense, structural deposits and NIM

38. Structural deposits can be understood as deposit-defense instruments whose embedded derivative changes customer yield and bank hedging economics.
39. Their headline customer rate is not necessarily comparable with plain time-deposit cost because option value and market-linked payoff matter.
40. Certificates of deposit and large-denomination deposits provide market-sensitive funding benchmarks even for banks with strong ordinary deposits.
41. Customer migration from deposits into WMPs may raise fee/AUM while increasing marginal funding cost; this is an internal cannibalization channel.
42. Corporate deposit beta can differ from household deposit beta because treasury customers optimize liquidity more aggressively.
43. Deposit mix matters: demand deposits, time deposits, structural deposits and institutional deposits can move in opposite directions.
44. NIM compression can be partly management choice if a bank accepts lower loan spreads to gain policy-favored or low-risk customers.
45. A lower asset yield paired with even lower funding cost can stabilize NIM; looking only at loan pricing misses liability management.
46. Late-period LPR changes should be separated from legacy benchmark-linked back-book repricing; immediate full-book effects are unlikely.

## Inclusive / micro finance economics

47. Inclusive lending can be commercially viable when digital underwriting lowers acquisition and monitoring costs; it should not automatically be modeled as policy subsidy.
48. Small-loan pricing includes operational cost and expected loss, so higher nominal yields do not necessarily mean higher risk-adjusted returns.
49. Digital tax/invoice/payment data can reduce information asymmetry but create model dependence and data-access risk.
50. Centralized scoring may reduce branch discretion, while local relationship knowledge remains useful for fraud and business-quality assessment.
51. Policy targets can produce end-period balance incentives or classification gaming if managers are judged mechanically on growth.
52. A bank may satisfy inclusive targets by moving existing qualifying borrowers into a reporting category rather than only by originating new borrowers.
53. Rapid growth can temporarily improve ratios before losses season; vintage analysis is essential.
54. ABC's county network may lower physical distribution cost for rural inclusive customers but can also preserve higher fixed branch costs.
55. ICBC/CCB digital scale may allow centralized micro-lending models with different acquisition economics from ABC's rural relationship model.

## Digital competition and failed mechanisms

63. Payment-platform competition erodes transaction visibility and daily engagement before it necessarily removes bank deposits.
64. Banks may respond with app ecosystems, merchant acquiring, online lending and wealth integration; success should be judged by active use and economics, not downloads.
65. Bank e-commerce ventures are plausible dead ends where banks had capital and customers but lacked platform network effects.
66. Branch "smart transformation" can reduce teller transactions while increasing advisory/sales intensity and conduct risk.
67. Digital acquisition can lower marginal cost but increase marketing subsidies and fraud losses, offsetting apparent efficiency.
68. API/open-banking partnerships may shift banks from owning interfaces to supplying regulated balance-sheet services behind third-party platforms.
69. Losing the customer interface can weaken deposit stickiness and cross-sell even when loan assets remain on the bank balance sheet.
