## Broad blind-memory leads

### 1. Funding, pricing and franchise
1. Interest-rate liberalization changed rural-bank competition from primarily quantity/franchise competition toward explicit liability pricing competition; county deposits became a more actively defended franchise rather than a passive funding base.
2. Deposit-rate competition likely intensified most where RCBs faced state-bank county branches, postal-bank outlets and stronger city banks in nearby prefectures.
3. Smaller RCC/RCB entities could retain sticky local deposits through payroll, village/SME settlement, public-sector relationships and physical access even while headline deposit pricing became more competitive.
4. Larger and more market-oriented RCBs increasingly had reason to supplement deposits with interbank borrowing, repo, negotiable certificates or other market liabilities; exact instrument adoption and dates require Evidence.
5. Negotiable certificates of deposit are an important search key for the strongest RCB cohort because they changed the marginal funding curve and made liability duration/market confidence more visible.
6. Wholesale funding should not be projected uniformly across rural banks: many county institutions likely remained overwhelmingly deposit funded while a smaller upper tier became materially more market funded.
7. Deposit insurance plausibly altered perceived small-bank safety and competitive parity, but its effect was mediated by local-government reputation, provincial-union backing, sponsor-bank reputation and household awareness.
8. A contemporaneous expectation worth testing is that deposit insurance would reduce implicit-guarantee distortions; a counter-lead is that retail customers may have continued to price local political/support signals more heavily than formal insurance mechanics.
9. Deposits, AUM-like customer funds and bank-issued wealth products should be kept semantically separate; apparent deposit loss could partly reflect migration into wealth products rather than franchise exit.
10. Rural-bank liability structure should be read with bank-only versus consolidated/group perimeter caution where wealth, financial-market or village-bank affiliates are involved.

### 2. Interbank, investment and shadow-credit adaptation
11. Stronger RCBs increasingly used bond books, interbank assets and broader investment portfolios as alternatives to plain county lending and as outlets for surplus deposits.
12. Some rural banks likely adopted structures analogous to investment receivables, trust beneficiary rights, asset-management plans or channel products used elsewhere in the banking system; these are leads, not universal BANK-RURAL features.
13. “应收款项类投资/应收款项类金融资产” is an old-accounting/search key worth preserving because it can hide credit-like exposure from a simple loan-only view.
14. Interbank and investment growth can mean two opposite things: excess-deposit placement by a locally liquid bank, or funding-assisted asset expansion by a more aggressive bank; the sign cannot be inferred from the asset label alone.
15. A high investment-to-loan share is therefore not automatically evidence of weak local lending demand, nor automatically evidence of shadow-credit arbitrage.
16. Rural-bank exposure to trust/channel structures may have connected local property, LGFV-like, industrial-cluster or out-of-area credit risks to banks whose legal franchise remained county or regional.
17. Off-balance-sheet wealth-management growth, if present, complicates the boundary between on-balance-sheet funding, fee income and retained credit risk.
18. “同业业务”, “同业投资”, “买入返售”, “应收款项类投资”, “非标”, “理财资金”, “通道” and “资产管理计划” are period vocabulary leads to test later.
19. The upper tail of RCBs may have converged partly toward city-bank balance-sheet techniques even while their deposit franchise and governance lineage remained rural.
20. Counterexample: a bank with almost no material wholesale/shadow expansion could still be a successful RCB if its county deposit franchise and conventional loan book were strong.

### 3. Provincial-union control versus legal-person autonomy
21. Provincial rural-credit unions likely became more important as treasury, clearing, technology, product, risk-management and sometimes personnel/governance platforms across legally separate county entities.
22. Provincial-union liquidity pooling or emergency support can make an individual rural bank look more standalone on the legal chart than it is economically during stress.
23. Centralized core banking, payments, data warehouses and risk systems created quasi-group operating scale without necessarily creating a single consolidated banking legal person.
24. The province-level platform could compress unit technology cost and speed product rollout, but could also propagate common models, correlated exposures and governance weaknesses across many affiliates.
25. Personnel appointment, senior-management influence and strategic approval are distinct from treasury/IT service provision; provincial-union “control” needs functional decomposition rather than one binary label.
26. A county RCB conversion did not necessarily eliminate provincial-union influence; legal conversion and effective control could diverge.
27. “省联社—县级法人” should therefore be modeled as a layered governance architecture, not as either full independence or a conventional bank holding group.
28. Regional reform heterogeneity matters: some provinces may have stronger centralized coordination, others greater county-bank autonomy or alternative provincial platforms.
29. A future Evidence test should distinguish provincial-union assets/liabilities from member-bank assets/liabilities to avoid double counting or false group ratios.
30. Contemporaneous judgment lead: policymakers may have expected provincial coordination to professionalize small banks; the later counter-question is whether concentrated administrative control sometimes delayed genuine shareholder/board accountability.

### 4. Credit mix, local economy and asset migration
31. Agriculture and household production lending remained core, but the economic meaning of “三农” could broaden toward rural SMEs, county industry, agribusiness, commerce and nonfarm household activity.
32. County SME/private-enterprise clusters likely became a larger earnings source for commercialized RCBs, often with collateral, guarantee-company or mutual-guarantee dependence.
33. Property-linked credit could enter through local developers, construction firms, land-backed local companies, homebuyers, building-material suppliers or guarantee chains rather than only direct developer loans.
34. Local-government/public-company exposure should not be equated mechanically with formal LGFV classification; county infrastructure companies, public utilities and land-related entities may sit on a spectrum.
35. Some richer-county RCBs likely became less “agricultural” economically even while remaining BANK-RURAL by lineage and regulatory classification.
36. Coastal manufacturing counties, resource regions, agricultural provinces and peri-urban counties should be treated as separate macro/portfolio archetypes.
37. Export-manufacturing counties could face SME and guarantee-chain stress unrelated to farm cycles; inland agricultural banks could face weather/commodity/household-income risks with very different correlation structure.
38. Peri-urban RCBs could become materially exposed to property, land and urbanization despite rural lineage.
39. The strongest RCBs may have expanded beyond their original county or into adjacent urban markets, but cross-region expansion should not automatically reclassify them as BANK-CITY.
40. Counterexample: weaker local economies could produce lower loan demand and more securities placement, so a high investment share may coexist with conservative rather than aggressive behavior.

### 5. Post-stimulus seasoning and asset-quality semantics
41. 2013-2016 is a likely seasoning window for loans originated during the earlier stimulus/rapid-growth period; vintage matters more than contemporaneous origination volume alone.
42. NPL ratios can look stable because loan growth changes the denominator, write-offs/disposals remove stock, restructurings delay recognition, or overdue loans sit outside NPL classification.
43. “关注类贷款”, “逾期贷款”, “逾期90天以上”, “不良贷款”, “重组贷款”, “借新还旧” and “展期” are distinct semantic buckets and should not be merged.
44. The gap between overdue and NPL measures is a useful indicator lead for recognition discipline but requires exact definitions and period-specific reporting standards.
45. Guarantee-company failure or mutual-guarantee contagion can convert many individually small SME exposures into a correlated regional loss event.
46. Local collateral values can be endogenous to the same county downturn that triggers defaults; land, factories and commercial property may all lose liquidation value together.
47. Rural-bank NPL cleanup may include cash collection, restructuring, write-off, transfer, shareholder/local-government support or other disposal channels; the apparent ratio alone does not identify economic loss.
48. Provision coverage can improve while underlying new-vintage risk worsens if the denominator or recognition policy changes; ratio interpretation needs stock-flow reconciliation.
49. Capital adequacy after rapid expansion depends on both retained earnings and risk-weighted asset growth, not just nominal capital injections.
50. Small-bank capital instruments and shareholder replenishment capacity may be more constrained than at large banks, making local fiscal/shareholder quality an important resilience variable.

### 6. Village banks and other rural-institution lineages
51. Village banks had moved from pure pilot novelty toward a more mature sponsor-led network in some regions, but remained structurally distinct from RCC-to-RCB conversions.
52. Sponsor-bank governance can provide systems, risk standards, personnel and liquidity credibility, but can also create distance from local minority shareholders or customers.
53. “主发起行” is a key vocabulary/search lead; sponsor capacity and incentives should be separated from the village bank’s own legal capital and local franchise.
54. Cold-start deposit acquisition remained a structural challenge: a new village bank lacked the inherited deposit network of an RCC and could depend heavily on sponsor reputation, local shareholders or higher pricing.
55. Village-bank balance-sheet growth should not be aggregated with sponsor-bank growth without clear perimeter rules.
56. Sponsor-bank distress or strategic retrenchment could later become a transmission channel to multiple village-bank affiliates even if each affiliate was separately licensed.
57. Counterexample: a village bank can be locally profitable and well governed without ever reaching system-scale importance; lineage distinctness is not the same as period-wide model dominance.
58. Rural mutual/cooperative or microcredit entities outside licensed banking remain boundary checks rather than BANK-RURAL institutions unless the legal form actually falls inside the manifest scope.

### 7. Mobile payments, channels and customer economics
59. Smartphone adoption and third-party mobile payment platforms began to erode the exclusivity of the physical branch/account relationship even in county and rural markets.
60. Alipay/WeChat-style payments are important channel-disruption leads because they can shift transaction frequency away from bank counters while leaving deposits temporarily sticky.
61. Mobile banking and province-wide digital platforms could let smaller rural banks offer basic payments and account services at much lower marginal channel cost.
62. Physical outlets could remain important for cash, elderly customers, government payments, relationship lending and deposit acquisition even as transaction traffic migrated online.
63. “网点少了 = franchise weakened” is therefore not a valid inference without transaction, deposit, customer and digital-use context.
64. Third-party payment penetration could weaken fee income and settlement data advantages before materially weakening deposits.
65. Rural remittances from migrant workers are a useful forgotten use case: mobile payments and cards can change which bank captures household flows and data.
66. Province-level common IT can allow a small county bank to present a digital interface disproportionate to its standalone technology budget.

### 8. Governance, ownership and incentive discontinuities
67. Commercialization and shareholding reform did not eliminate local-government or connected-shareholder influence; it changed the channels through which influence operated.
68. Related-party lending may move from direct administrative credit toward shareholder-linked companies, industrial groups, guarantee networks or hidden beneficial ownership.
69. Board formalization can coexist with dominant shareholder or local political influence; legal corporate governance is not the same as effective credit independence.
70. Rapid asset growth after conversion can create incentive pressure to preserve headline growth and low NPL ratios, increasing evergreen/restructuring or recognition risk.
71. Some leading RCBs likely entered listing preparation or public-market governance transitions around this period; exact actors and timing require Evidence.
72. Public listing or IPO preparation can improve disclosure and capital access but does not automatically solve local concentration or related-party risk.
73. Executive and employee incentive structures are a potential missing lead: commercialization may create stronger growth/profit incentives without equally mature risk controls.
74. Anti-corruption/discipline campaigns may have altered local-government-bank relationships, credit approval behavior and executive turnover in ways not visible in balance-sheet ratios.

### 9. Capital, regulation and metric breaks
75. Implementation of more risk-sensitive capital rules made RWA composition, off-balance-sheet conversion and capital-quality semantics more important for rural-bank comparisons.
76. CET1/core capital terminology, subordinated debt, retained earnings and shareholder injections should be kept distinct; exact regulatory definitions are Evidence-stage.
77. A bank growing low-risk securities can show different capital consumption from one growing SME/property credit even with similar asset growth.
78. Off-balance-sheet wealth and guarantees can create economic risk not proportional to reported loan balances.
79. Bank-only versus consolidated/group data become increasingly important when a rural-bank group owns village banks or other affiliates.
80. Conversion from RCC/cooperative-bank form to RCB form can create structural breaks in reported equity, shares, member ownership, provisioning and asset-quality series.
81. “股金” from cooperative antecedents should not be back-projected as equivalent to modern common equity without checking legal and loss-absorption terms.
82. Deposits per outlet, loans per employee and NIM can jump after mergers/conversions or channel migration even without comparable organic productivity gains.

### 10. Expectations, failures and counterexamples
83. Contemporaneous expectation lead: rate liberalization was often expected to reward well-managed local franchises; a counterexample is that aggressive small banks could use higher deposit pricing and market funding to grow faster before risk surfaced.
84. Contemporaneous expectation lead: shadow/interbank diversification could appear to reduce county concentration; a counterexample is hidden credit exposure to the same regional property/local-government cycle through investment structures.
85. Contemporaneous expectation lead: RCB conversion and modern boards could be read as governance completion; a counterexample is persistent local political/shareholder influence after legal-form modernization.
86. Contemporaneous expectation lead: village banks were designed to increase rural competition and access; a counter-lead is that weak deposit franchises or sponsor incentives could constrain independent local credit creation.
87. Contemporaneous expectation lead: digital channels should lower costs; a counterexample is simultaneous technology investment, cybersecurity/operations cost and continued need for dense rural service coverage.
88. Failure/dead-end lead: some aggressive investment/interbank strategies may have looked like sophistication before deleveraging exposed liquidity and valuation sensitivity.
89. Failure/dead-end lead: mutual guarantees and guarantee companies can transform relationship-lending networks into contagion networks when local industry turns down.
90. Failure/dead-end lead: capital injections can reset ratios without fixing underwriting, governance or concentration, leading to recurrent recapitalization need.
91. Regional counterexample: a deposit-rich agricultural RCB with low wholesale funding may remain stable but low-growth; a wealthy-county RCB may be high-growth and market-funded; a distressed industrial-county bank may have both weak deposits and legacy SME losses.
92. Another counterexample is a peri-urban RCB whose economic risk resembles a city bank while legal lineage, provincial-union ties and branch network remain distinctly rural.
