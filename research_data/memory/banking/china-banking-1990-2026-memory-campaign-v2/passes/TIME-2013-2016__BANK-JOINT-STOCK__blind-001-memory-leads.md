## Broad blind-memory leads

### Rate liberalization, pricing and deposit franchise
1. [new_category][high] Progressive rate liberalization turned the joint-stock-bank contest from benchmark-rate execution toward customer-level pricing, deposit-beta management and relationship economics; the timing and degree of pass-through likely varied materially by bank.
2. [new_category][high] Deposit competition became a strategic constraint rather than a passive balance-sheet residual; joint-stock banks with thinner branch/deposit franchises had stronger incentives to use market funding, wealth products or aggressive pricing.
3. [new_category] Internet money-market products and platform-based cash management likely accelerated household and corporate sensitivity to deposit opportunity cost, forcing banks to defend transactional balances with service, pricing and wealth offerings.
4. [new_category] The distinction between operational/settlement deposits and rate-sensitive investment balances became more important for funding durability.
5. [useful_refinement] Average deposit cost, marginal deposit cost and headline offered rate are different semantic objects; rapid franchise changes can make period-end deposit ratios misleading.
6. [new_category] FTP increasingly mattered as a management mechanism for allocating liquidity and interest-rate costs across corporate, retail, treasury and branch units rather than merely as an accounting transfer.
7. [new_category] Relationship pricing could cross-subsidize low-yield lending with settlement, payroll, custody, cards, wealth or trade-finance economics; loan spread alone may understate franchise profitability.
8. [new_category] Rate liberalization could compress legacy deposit-spread advantages while rewarding banks with better customer segmentation, transaction flows and wealth conversion.
9. [useful_refinement] NIM compression should be decomposed into asset yield, deposit cost, wholesale-funding mix, repricing lag, fee substitution and balance-sheet composition rather than treated as a single price effect.
10. [counterexample] A bank with faster deposit-cost increase need not have a weaker franchise if it was deliberately replacing unstable wholesale liabilities with customer deposits or moving into richer retail relationships.

### Interbank CDs, wholesale funding and liquidity
11. [new_category][high] Negotiable interbank certificates of deposit became an important funding instrument for banks that wanted scalable market liabilities beyond traditional interbank borrowing and customer deposits.
12. [new_category][high] Some joint-stock banks likely built materially larger wholesale/interbank liability shares than deposit-rich peers, creating a structural funding-model divide inside the cohort.
13. [new_category] Interbank CDs changed liability maturity and pricing visibility, making marginal wholesale funding costs more market-sensitive and potentially more procyclical.
14. [new_category] Interbank assets and liabilities should be viewed jointly: a bank can appear large on both sides because it acts as a market intermediary rather than simply because it is a net liquidity taker.
15. [new_category][high] The 2013 interbank liquidity shock is a bank-behavior stress test for reliance on short-term market funding, internal liquidity buffers and treasury governance; system-policy chronology belongs to the sealed system shard.
16. [new_category] Treasury desks and ALM committees became more consequential as funding markets, duration, liquidity and investment books expanded relative to simple loan-deposit banking.
17. [new_category] Wholesale funding enabled rapid asset growth when branch deposits lagged, but it could also increase sensitivity to market confidence and liquidity repricing.
18. [useful_refinement] Gross interbank liabilities, net interbank position and short-term wholesale-funding dependence are not interchangeable metrics.
19. [new_category] Funding diversification can be genuinely risk-reducing when maturities and counterparties are diversified, even if the wholesale-funding share rises.
20. [counterexample] A high interbank-liability share is not by itself evidence of fragility; matched liquid assets, strong capital and diversified counterparties can change the interpretation.

### Nonstandard assets, receivable-type investments and channel structures
21. [new_category][high] Joint-stock banks were among the banks most associated in memory with rapid growth in non-loan credit-like assets booked through investment or receivable-type categories.
22. [new_category][high] “应收款项类投资” / receivable-type investment is an important period vocabulary lead because economic credit exposure could migrate from loans into investment-accounting categories.
23. [new_category] Trust beneficiary rights, directional asset-management plans and other channel structures may have been used to hold credit-like exposures with different accounting or prudential treatment; exact structures require later evidence.
24. [new_category] Securities-firm, fund-subsidiary and trust channels increasingly formed a product-manufacturing and asset-holding ecosystem around bank-originated or bank-distributed credit exposure.
25. [new_category] “非标” should be treated as an economic-asset class with multiple legal wrappers rather than as one homogeneous product.
26. [new_category] Buy-back/repo-style and interbank-investment structures may have served liquidity, investment or credit-transformation purposes; the legal form cannot be mapped mechanically to economic substance.
27. [new_category][high] Rapid asset expansion can therefore be missed if analysts compare only loan growth; loan + credit-like investment + off-balance-sheet origination is a more relevant conceptual perimeter.
28. [useful_refinement] Reclassification from loans to investments can lower apparent loan ratios without reducing underlying borrower or sector risk.
29. [new_category] Look-through analysis should distinguish originator, funder, distributor, manager, custodian and final risk bearer; one bank can occupy several roles in the same chain.
30. [counterexample] Not every nonstandard or interbank investment represented regulatory arbitrage; some structures provided genuine liquidity, client investment access or market intermediation.

### Wealth management and customer-asset migration
31. [new_category][high] Bank wealth-management products were mature enough in this slice to matter for customer-asset migration, fee income and funding competition, but they should not be backcast as identical to post-2018 net-asset-value wealth subsidiaries.
32. [new_category] WMPs could simultaneously relieve deposit-price constraints, retain customer assets within the bank franchise and create implicit-support or liquidity expectations.
33. [new_category] Wealth-product maturities and underlying asset maturities may have differed, making rollover and liquidity behavior analytically relevant even before later regulatory reform.
34. [new_category] Wealth-management economics varied by role: manufacturing spread, distribution fee, custody, advisory, channel fee and customer retention should be separated.
35. [new_category] A strong wealth franchise could weaken reported deposit growth while strengthening total customer financial assets and fee economics.
36. [useful_refinement] Deposit migration into WMPs is not necessarily franchise loss; migration to another institution or platform is a different economic event.
37. [new_category] Private banking and affluent wealth services became an increasingly visible differentiator among national joint-stock banks.
38. [new_category] Corporate wealth and cash-management products may have blurred the boundary between transaction deposits and investment balances for enterprise customers.
39. [counterexample] High wealth-product issuance need not imply weak deposits; leading deposit franchises may also have the strongest wealth-distribution capability.
40. [uncertain_fragment] Some banks may already have used product structures later associated with “资金池” or maturity transformation; exact period terminology and institution mapping need evidence verification.

### Asset quality: property, LGFV, overcapacity and private enterprise
41. [new_category][high] The 2009-2012 credit vintages likely seasoned into visible asset-quality pressure during 2013-2016; NPL formation should be read as a cohort process rather than a contemporaneous lending decision alone.
42. [new_category][high] Overcapacity sectors such as steel, coal, shipbuilding or related industrial chains were important stress archetypes for corporate books; exact bank-sector concentrations require later evidence.
43. [new_category] Trade-finance and collateral chains around commodities/steel could convert apparently short-duration working-capital credit into concentrated fraud, collateral or guarantee risk.
44. [new_category] Private-enterprise guarantee circles and mutual-guarantee chains can transmit localized stress across otherwise unrelated borrowers.
45. [new_category] Property exposure must be separated into developer credit, mortgages, construction-related firms, land-related counterparties and wealth/investment holdings.
46. [new_category] LGFV/local-government exposure could migrate among loans, bonds and investment products; legal form may change while fiscal repayment dependence remains.
47. [new_category] Local-government debt-swap activity can reduce bank credit risk yet also compress asset yield or alter duration; “risk improvement” and “profitability improvement” are not equivalent.
48. [new_category] Supply-side capacity reduction and SOE restructuring toward the end of the slice could create both recognition pressure and eventual cleanup opportunities for banks.
49. [useful_refinement] Sector NPL ratios are affected by denominator contraction as banks exit sectors; rising NPL ratio can coexist with falling exposure.
50. [counterexample] A bank reporting faster NPL recognition may be cleaning up more aggressively rather than experiencing uniquely worse underlying credit quality.

### NPL, provisioning, capital and accounting semantics
51. [new_category][high] NPL formation flow, ending NPL balance, NPL ratio, overdue loans, special-mention loans and restructured exposures are separate measures that can diverge materially.
52. [new_category] Write-offs and disposals can stabilize the reported NPL balance while gross formation remains elevated; net change is therefore not the same as credit deterioration.
53. [new_category] Provision coverage and reserve-to-loan ratios answer different questions; a high coverage ratio can partly reflect a low NPL denominator.
54. [new_category] Credit cost is a flow through the income statement and should be separated from the stock of loan-loss reserves.
55. [new_category] Pre-provision operating profit became an important capacity measure for absorbing credit costs during an NPL upcycle.
56. [new_category] Basel-style capital composition and RWA density increasingly mattered for judging whether rapid asset growth was capital-efficient or capital-hungry.
57. [new_category] Preference shares, subordinated instruments or other capital tools became relevant funding/capital leads for listed banks; exact issue timing and bank mapping require later verification.
58. [useful_refinement] Headline capital ratios can improve through new capital issuance, lower RWA density or slower asset growth without any change in underlying profitability.
59. [new_category] Loan securitization or other asset-distribution mechanisms may have re-emerged as tools for balance-sheet and capital management, but the scale and institution mapping are uncertain.
60. [counterexample] Falling provision coverage is not automatically evidence of weaker prudence if NPL recognition rises faster than reserve accumulation during cleanup.

62. [new_category][high] China Merchants Bank is remembered as a stronger retail/wealth/private-banking and customer-franchise archetype; the CMB 2023 preexposure carveout means directly overlapping later management language is not counted as blind support.

64. [new_category] Ping An Bank’s integration and group affiliation likely supported a more explicit retail/cross-sell/technology strategy, but the pace and realized economics need period evidence.
65. [new_category] SPDB retained a strong corporate/urban franchise orientation and could differ from peers in wholesale, Shanghai/Yangtze-region and large-corporate exposure.

69. [useful_refinement] “Joint-stock bank” peer averages can obscure radically different deposit franchises, market-funding dependence, retail intensity and asset mix.
70. [counterexample] A smaller joint-stock bank can have better franchise quality than a larger peer if deposits, customer activity and risk-adjusted returns are stronger.

### Retail, cards, digital and channel economics
71. [new_category] Credit cards became a material retail-credit and payments franchise for several joint-stock banks, but card receivables, transaction volume, fee income and customer acquisition should not be conflated.
72. [new_category] Retail mortgages and unsecured/consumer credit have different risk, funding and pricing profiles; “retail loan growth” is too coarse a category.
73. [new_category] Mobile banking and internet banking moved from supplementary channels toward primary customer interfaces, changing branch traffic and service economics.
74. [new_category] Third-party payment and internet-finance platforms challenged banks at the payment/traffic interface even when banks retained the underlying deposit account.
75. [new_category] Direct-bank or online-only banking experiments are a period lead, but some may have been strategic dead ends rather than durable business-model changes.
76. [new_category] Branch transformation could reduce transaction handling while increasing sales/advisory roles; branch count alone is an incomplete productivity measure.
77. [useful_refinement] Digital adoption can lower transaction cost but raise technology, fraud, cyber and model-governance requirements.
78. [counterexample] Strong digital growth does not necessarily imply branch irrelevance; complex corporate, wealth and credit relationships may remain relationship-manager intensive.

### Governance, expectations, failures and old vocabulary
79. [new_category] Rapid balance-sheet growth likely increased tension between business-line growth targets and centralized risk controls, especially where market funding allowed assets to scale faster than stable deposits.
80. [new_category] Board/shareholder expectations at listed joint-stock banks could favor ROE and growth while supervisors/risk teams pushed for capital, liquidity and asset-quality restraint.
81. [new_category] Internal limits on sector, geography, branch authority and counterparty concentration became increasingly important as national networks matured.
82. [old_vocabulary] Period search keys include “同业存单”, “同业业务”, “应收款项类投资”, “非标”, “银信合作”, “通道业务”, “理财资金池”, “关注类贷款”, “逾期贷款”, “拨备覆盖率”, “资本充足率”, “风险加权资产”.
83. [contemporaneous_expectations] A plausible contemporaneous bull case was that joint-stock banks could outgrow large banks through pricing flexibility, innovation, wealth and market-based funding; the bear case was that rapid asset growth and wholesale funding masked latent credit/liquidity risk.
84. [failed_or_restructured_institutions] Ping An Bank/SDB integration remains an important restructuring comparator for whether ownership/group change translates into durable franchise economics rather than just rebranding or capital support.
85. [uncertain_fragment] Exact boundaries among “同业投资”, “买入返售”, “应收款项类投资” and later standardized/nonstandard labels likely changed across banks and reporting periods; later evidence must pin definitions before quantitative comparison.
86. [counterexample][high] The cohort should not be narrated as a single shadow-banking trade: some joint-stock banks relied more on stable customer deposits/retail wealth, others on market funding and investment assets, and still others on corporate transaction banking; heterogeneity is itself the central lead.
