## Result summary

This challenge did **not** reach low marginal novelty. A material cross-time category remains underrepresented: the large state-owned bank **transaction-franchise / primary-bank relationship** built through settlement, cash management, payroll, collections, custody, trade and treasury operating flows. The economic object is not merely a deposit product or a corporate loan; it is the bank's position inside a customer's operating cash-flow graph. That position can create stable or operational deposits, information advantages, fee flows, cross-sell opportunities and defensive switching costs, and it can explain why nominally similar large banks had different customer economics.

The following are memory leads, not verified claims.

## A. Customer ownership and the “primary bank” relationship

1. Large state banks historically competed not only for loan assets but for the right to be the enterprise's **main settlement bank / primary banking relationship**; exact contemporary terminology varied and should be recovered later.
2. A corporate borrower could have a large loan from one bank while its payroll, settlement, collections and treasury operating accounts sat elsewhere; loan market share therefore need not equal customer-franchise strength.
3. State-owned enterprises and infrastructure clients often generated dense networks of upstream/downstream payments, payroll and project accounts that could make the relationship economically valuable beyond lending spread.
4. “Customer ownership” likely shifted from branch-local relationship managers toward head-office or group-wide coverage models for strategic clients, but timing and degree differed by bank.
5. Head-office strategic-client departments, key-account lists or comparable constructs are likely useful old vocabulary/search keys, but exact names should not be invented here.
6. Cross-regional corporate groups created a recurring tension between local branch ownership of revenue and group-wide customer coordination.
7. Internal transfer-pricing and revenue-allocation rules could determine whether one branch willingly ceded deposits or fee income to another branch serving the same corporate group.
8. Large-bank scale can be misleading if customer relationships are shallow and product-specific rather than anchored in operating flows.
9. A “deposit franchise” can be an outcome of being embedded in customer operations, not merely a consequence of paying a competitive deposit rate.
10. Conversely, a bank can originate substantial assets while lacking transactional primacy, producing weaker cross-sell and more rate-sensitive funding.

## B. Settlement, cash management and operating deposits

11. Corporate settlement and account services are a distinct franchise layer: incoming receivables, outgoing supplier payments, payroll, tax or fee payments, and internal group transfers can create recurring balances.
12. Cash-management services likely evolved from account/settlement convenience toward liquidity concentration, account hierarchies, sweeping or pooling-like functions; exact legal/product forms and dates require evidence.
13. State-bank relationships with public-sector entities may have generated administrative, fiscal, social-security, utility or other collection/payment flows; exact eligible account types and institutional arrangements must be verified.
14. Payroll agency business can link a corporate relationship to millions of retail salary accounts, creating a bridge from wholesale customer acquisition to retail deposits, cards, payments and wealth distribution.
15. Salary accounts may be economically valuable even when individual balances are low because they establish recurring inflows and digital/payment engagement.
16. Merchant acquiring, bill collection, utility payment and government-service payment functions can similarly create transaction flows that later support retail customer retention.
17. The historical importance of settlement networks may have been larger before mobile-platform competition because switching operational banking arrangements was costly.
18. Real-time and digital payment infrastructure can reduce some traditional switching costs while simultaneously increasing the value of API/enterprise connectivity and data integration.
19. Transaction banking should be separated analytically from “fee income”: some services may be priced cheaply or implicitly in exchange for deposits and broader wallet share.
20. The correct economic metric may therefore be relationship-level revenue and funding value rather than product-level fee yield.

## C. Deposits as franchise output, not a standalone product

21. Corporate current/demand deposits can reflect operating cash balances generated by settlement primacy; they may be less rate-sensitive than negotiated term deposits, but this should not be assumed uniformly.
22. Deposit beta and termization pressures can reveal whether a bank's supposed franchise is genuinely transactional or simply rate-driven balance gathering.
23. Periods of tight liquidity may expose which corporate deposits are operationally sticky versus mobile treasury balances.
24. A bank with broad government/SOE settlement relationships may appear to have a low funding cost for structural reasons that are not replicable by a smaller or more retail-oriented bank.
25. Retail demand deposits can likewise be tied to salary, payment and card relationships rather than branch proximity alone.
26. Wealth-management growth can both deepen customer relationships and drain conventional deposits, making “customer assets” a better franchise concept than deposits alone in some periods.
27. Deposit market share can rise because of transaction flows, branch footprint, administrative relationships, pricing, wealth redemptions or risk-off migration; causal interpretation requires period-specific decomposition.
28. The same headline deposit growth can therefore represent very different franchise economics across banks and cycles.

## D. Corporate franchise beyond plain lending

29. Transaction banking / cash management likely became an explicit strategic business line as interest-rate liberalization and NIM pressure increased the value of non-credit corporate services.
30. Trade finance, guarantees, letters of credit, settlement and FX services can embed a bank in a corporate customer's working-capital cycle without necessarily expanding conventional loans proportionally.
31. Supply-chain finance can be understood as a franchise extension from an anchor client's transaction graph, not only as a credit product.
32. Large SOE or infrastructure relationships may provide access to suppliers, contractors and employees, creating network acquisition effects.
33. Custody and institutional services can reinforce corporate/institutional relationships even when the economics sit partly outside the lending book.
34. Treasury services—FX, rates, bond distribution, liquidity products—may deepen sophisticated corporate relationships and create fee/trading/flow revenue distinct from loan spread.
35. Bond-market development can weaken the bank's monopoly over corporate financing while preserving or even increasing the value of settlement, underwriting/distribution support, cash management and investor services.
36. A state bank that loses loan share to bond financing may still retain customer primacy if it controls operating accounts and capital-markets service relationships.
37. Conversely, balance-sheet growth can obscure franchise weakness if lending is policy-led or price-led and does not translate into customer wallet share.

## E. Retail franchise and channel economics

38. Branch networks historically created trust, cash-handling and customer-acquisition advantages, especially for older clients, public-sector payrolls, county markets and complex service interactions.
39. The economic value of a dense branch network should be decomposed into deposit gathering, service, sales, brand presence and operational cost rather than treated as uniformly positive.
40. ATM/card networks were once important scale advantages before mobile payment ecosystems reduced their differentiation.
41. Credit cards could create payment frequency and data but also generate acquisition, rewards and credit-cost burdens; “card scale” does not automatically mean superior franchise economics.
42. Mobile banking can shift relationship ownership from the physical branch to the bank's digital interface, but platform super-apps may intermediate customer attention.
43. Digital acquisition can reduce servicing cost while increasing price transparency and making low-engagement deposits more mobile.
44. Large banks may retain an advantage from installed salary/account relationships even when front-end payment engagement migrates to fintech platforms.
45. Rural/county network economics differ across the six large banks; PSBC's postal/agency legacy is an obvious counterexample to assuming all branch networks have the same cost and acquisition model.
46. ABC's county/rural positioning is another counterexample to treating retail franchise as a purely urban mass-affluent or mortgage story.
47. BOC's historically stronger cross-border/trade orientation is a counterexample to treating all large-bank corporate franchises as domestically homogeneous.
48. CCB's historical association with construction/infrastructure and ICBC's broad industrial/commercial footprint are useful heterogeneity leads, but exact comparative claims require later verification.

## F. Wealth, custody and “customer assets” versus deposits

49. A mature retail franchise can migrate from deposits toward wealth, funds, insurance and custody/distribution relationships while the bank still retains customer ownership.
50. This creates a semantic break between “deposit customer” and broader “AUM/customer assets” concepts; definitions and consolidation perimeter vary by bank and period.
51. Wealth-management subsidiaries further separate product manufacturing from bank distribution, making group-versus-bank perimeter and customer-channel ownership distinct questions.
52. A bank may report fee pressure even as customer assets rise if fee rates compress or product mix shifts; franchise depth should not be inferred from fee growth alone.
53. Private banking is likely a relationship-deepening layer rather than simply a wealth-product category, combining deposits, investment products, credit, trust/insurance coordination and family/business needs.
54. Corporate-owner and entrepreneur relationships can link corporate banking and private banking, but this pattern may be more visible at joint-stock banks than at state banks and should be treated as a comparative lead only.

## G. Strategic narratives and counterexamples

55. “Comprehensive financial services”, “integrated finance”, “light-capital transformation”, “customer-centric transformation”, “ecosystem” and similar slogans may appear across periods; the key research question is which changed economics versus merely vocabulary.
56. A shift from product-centered to customer-centered management is plausible as a recurring strategy theme, but organizational incentives can continue to reward product volumes, creating a gap between narrative and execution.
57. Cross-sell ratios or “customers holding N products” type metrics, if used, require careful definition and may not be comparable across banks or time.
58. Network reduction or branch optimization need not mean franchise erosion if digital servicing and transaction connectivity substitute effectively; the opposite can also occur in segments dependent on physical presence.
59. A bank can have strong deposit share yet weak fee/AUM economics if the relationship is administrative or low-engagement rather than advisory or transactional in a broad sense.
60. A bank can have high fee income yet weak core deposits if fee businesses are market-sensitive or distributed without durable operating-account primacy.
61. Policy-driven credit expansion can temporarily increase customer counts and assets without proving durable franchise quality.
62. Crisis or risk-off periods can temporarily strengthen large state-bank deposits because of perceived safety; such inflows should not automatically be labeled structural customer-franchise gains.
63. Similar balance-sheet scale across two state banks can conceal different shares of low-cost operating deposits, wealth assets, payment activity, institutional custody and transaction-service revenue.
64. The economically relevant “franchise” may therefore be a multi-layer system: operating flows → information → balances → product access → cross-sell → switching cost → risk-adjusted relationship profitability.
65. The missing material category is not another product silo but this **transaction-franchise / customer-operating-flow layer** that connects products and explains why deposit and asset franchises differ across large state banks.
