## Orthogonal falsification question

Does the late-1990s rural-finance landscape require a second period-wide licensed BANK-RURAL model beyond an RCC-centered transition system? The independent challenge below tests six possible fracture lines without replaying the broad chronology.

## A. Formal RCC–ABC separation versus effective dependence

1. Formal separation from ABC should not be equated with immediate operational self-sufficiency.
2. Settlement, clearing, cash supply, technology, staff capability and liquidity management could remain partly dependent on legacy arrangements or public-sector coordination after formal management links changed.
3. A useful distinction is **administrative parentage** versus **economic infrastructure dependence**.
4. County unions likely served as a practical bridge between many small grassroots RCCs and higher-level supervisory/liquidity institutions.
5. If county unions centralized clearing but not all assets/liabilities, apparent “county RCC” statistics may blend management aggregation and legal-entity data.
6. Separation could initially weaken rather than strengthen controls if ABC systems/personnel exited before replacement supervisory capacity matured.
7. Local PBOC branches plausibly became important in supervisory coordination and liquidity support, but exact responsibilities are Evidence-stage.
8. Personnel continuity matters: a change in formal reporting line does not imply a rapid change in underwriting or internal-control culture.
9. The strongest structural interpretation remains one RCC system undergoing governance reassignment, not two separate licensed rural-bank archetypes.

## B. County governance, member shares and historical-loss allocation

10. Cooperative-member shares are best treated as a governance/capital instrument with historically specific withdrawal and control characteristics, not as modern common equity.
11. Member ownership could be diffuse while actual influence concentrated in managers, local cadres, major enterprises or government-linked actors.
12. The county-union layer could coordinate institutions without solving principal-agent problems at grassroots outlets.
13. A local government that influenced lending could later also be asked to help resolve losses, creating circular incentives.
14. “Who caused the historical loss?” and “who ultimately absorbs it?” are distinct questions.
15. Historical-loss allocation may have been politically contested across grassroots RCCs, county unions, local governments, members and central/public support channels.
16. Recapitalization through new member shares could cosmetically improve nominal capital while failing to remove old bad assets.
17. Capital adequacy concepts were likely not directly comparable with later Basel-style measures; old net-worth/member-share ratios require semantic checking.
18. Local fiscal weakness is a structural constraint on any decentralized cleanup model: poor counties have less capacity to backstop the same cooperative institution.
19. This creates regional severity differences inside one governance model, not a second model.

## C. Rural cooperative foundations and liability migration

20. Rural cooperative foundations and similar local quasi-financial vehicles are a strong falsification candidate because they could look bank-like to depositors.
21. Their legal/regulatory perimeter is nevertheless the decisive distinction: deposit-like fundraising and local lending do not automatically make them licensed RCCs.
22. Cleanup of such entities could transmit losses into the formal rural system through depositor migration, local-government support, asset transfers or confidence effects.
23. Formal RCCs might receive deposits after a foundation closure while simultaneously inheriting weaker local borrowers or political pressure to assist resolution.
24. Liability assumption, asset assumption and simple depositor migration must be kept distinct.
25. A failed foundation can create liquidity pressure at neighboring RCCs even if no legal liabilities are transferred.
26. Local authorities might use negotiated repayment or fiscal resources to preserve stability; that is not equivalent to formal bank resolution.
27. Quasi-finance cleanup therefore behaves as an **adjacent-system shock and balance-sheet migration mechanism**, not a second licensed BANK-RURAL institution class.
28. Informal lenders, rotating savings arrangements and private credit likewise belong to boundary analysis unless a licensed rural bank is directly involved.

## D. TVE restructuring, legal-obligor continuity and macro stress

29. Late-1990s TVE/collective-enterprise stress is capable of producing large RCC asset-quality deterioration without changing the institution model.
30. Privatization or restructuring can sever the link between the original legal borrower and the surviving productive assets.
31. A nominally “collective” borrower may become privately controlled while old debt remains unresolved.
32. Local-government sponsorship may reduce willingness to enforce aggressively against employers/tax bases.
33. Guarantee chains among local firms can transform idiosyncratic borrower stress into county-wide correlated losses.
34. Deflation matters because nominal revenue and collateral values weaken while nominal debt service remains sticky.
35. Asian-crisis transmission should be regionally segmented: export-oriented coastal counties versus inland agricultural counties.
36. Rural borrowers with little direct foreign exposure could still be hit through prices, employment, remittances or local industrial chains.
37. Agricultural households and TVEs should be separated in analysis; both may sit in RCC portfolios but have different cash-flow drivers.
38. Rollovers can conceal borrower distress during restructuring; a stable loan balance may mask deteriorating economic quality.
39. Legal-obligor continuity is a high-value Evidence key when studying enterprise restructuring and bad-loan recovery.
40. These mechanisms point to portfolio concentration and governance weakness inside the RCC-centered model, not a second licensed archetype.

## E. Postal savings, local funds outflow and liquidity support

41. Postal savings is structurally different from a competing rural bank because its classic issue is where mobilized rural deposits are redeployed.
42. A county can simultaneously exhibit high household saving and local credit scarcity if savings leave the local intermediation circuit.
43. RCC deposit competition therefore has both a pricing dimension and a geographic funds-retention dimension.
44. Funds outflow may increase the value of central-bank refinancing or other liquidity channels for seasonal rural lending.
45. Seasonal agricultural credit demand can create short-term funding needs even where annual deposit growth is healthy.
46. County liquidity pooling may reduce idiosyncratic outlet stress but also spread losses or weaken market discipline.
47. Deposit confidence after local quasi-financial failures could cause flight toward institutions perceived as more state-backed, regardless of nominal yields.
48. “High deposit growth” should not be used as evidence of strong franchise without checking whether deposits are stable, retained locally and economically profitable.
49. Central-bank support can stabilize liquidity without resolving insolvency; liquidity and historical-loss cleanup must remain separate.
50. Postal-savings outflow and public liquidity support are external funding mechanisms around the same RCC institution class.

## F. Old asset-quality, rollover and consolidation semantics

51. “一逾两呆” remains a period-specific semantic regime; its categories should not be mapped mechanically onto later five-category NPLs.
52. An apparent jump in bad loans around classification reform may be partly a recognition effect.
53. A loan can be economically impaired before crossing an old overdue/sluggish/bad threshold.
54. Rollovers/extensions may delay overdue status and should be separated from genuinely performing new loans.
55. Interest accrual conventions may materially affect reported income; cash collection is a safer economic anchor than booked interest where systems are weak.
56. Provisioning and write-off capacity depend on capital and accounting rules, so weak write-offs can leave large legacy stocks on balance sheet.
57. Member shares should not be mixed with deposits when assessing liability stability.
58. Grassroots RCC, county union and any combined reporting perimeter need explicit identification before comparing assets, deposits or NPL ratios.
59. A merged/closed outlet may disappear from institution counts without a corresponding disappearance of its assets or liabilities.
60. “Cleanup” can mean recognition, transfer, write-off, recapitalization, collection campaign or administrative restructuring; the term alone is not an economic outcome.
61. “保支付” is primarily a confidence/liquidity objective and does not prove solvency.
62. “清产核资” is an inventory/valuation step, not itself loss absorption.
63. “增资扩股” can improve nominal capital while leaving governance or asset quality unchanged.
64. “消化历史包袱” is outcome-oriented vocabulary whose actual mechanism must be identified claim by claim.

## G. Boundary and counterexample checks

65. ABC remains BANK-STATE-OWNED even when it lends to rural households or agriculture; borrower sector does not determine bank-type shard.
66. Urban credit cooperatives remain city-bank lineage, not BANK-RURAL, despite the shared “credit cooperative” label.
67. Rural cooperative foundations remain adjacent quasi-finance unless a specific licensed transformation is established.
68. Later rural commercial banks and rural cooperative banks must not be projected backward as if they were already the dominant late-1990s legal forms.
69. A prosperous coastal RCC can look commercially sophisticated while still sharing the same institutional lineage as a weak inland RCC.
70. A poor-county RCC may depend more heavily on public liquidity/support, but dependence intensity does not create a distinct legal/business-model class.
71. A county dominated by TVEs can have SME-like portfolio economics without becoming a city commercial bank.
72. A county dominated by agriculture can have seasonal household-credit economics without becoming a policy bank.
73. Adjacent failures can alter liabilities and confidence without changing the licensed institution taxonomy.

## H. Failed expectations / dead ends

74. Formal separation from ABC may have been expected to produce autonomy; actual dependence on public clearing/liquidity and local governance could persist.
75. Cooperative ownership may have been expected to discipline lending; dispersed member control and local capture could blunt that mechanism.
76. Local information may have been expected to reduce bad loans; correlated TVE/local-government exposure could overwhelm information advantages.
77. Cleaning up quasi-financial foundations may have been expected to remove disorder; losses and depositor expectations could migrate into formal institutions/public balance sheets.
78. Increased member shares may have been expected to solve capital weakness; historical-loss stocks could overwhelm incremental capital.
79. Better classification may have been interpreted as worsening credit quality; part of the deterioration may instead be recognition of old problems.
80. Rural savings growth may have been expected to fund rural development; postal/funds-outflow structures could break that link.

## I. Uncertain fragments for later Evidence

82. Exact county-union legal and accounting perimeter by province.
83. Exact cleanup mechanisms for rural cooperative foundations and whether any liabilities/assets were formally transferred to RCCs.
84. Timing of classification-rule adoption by rural institutions.
85. Specific central-bank refinancing facilities and eligibility rules for RCCs.
86. Accounting treatment and withdrawal rights attached to member shares.
87. Specific early pilots of rural cooperative-bank/commercial-bank organizational forms before the 2000s reform wave.
88. Regional differences in how TVE restructuring debts were recognized or collected.
89. Specific postal-savings funds-flow arrangements and any early local-return experiments.
90. Whether particular county unions centralized treasury/clearing before later provincial reform structures.

## Orthogonal disposition

No second period-wide licensed BANK-RURAL structural category emerged. The independent challenge supports a single RCC-centered transition model with multiple mechanisms: post-ABC governance reassignment, county coordination, local administrative capture, adjacent quasi-finance cleanup, TVE/collective-enterprise asset stress, postal-savings funds outflow, public liquidity support, and historically specific capital/asset-quality semantics. The 1995–1999 slice can therefore be marked broad+orthogonal complete and the next frontier should advance to 2000–2004 broad recall.
