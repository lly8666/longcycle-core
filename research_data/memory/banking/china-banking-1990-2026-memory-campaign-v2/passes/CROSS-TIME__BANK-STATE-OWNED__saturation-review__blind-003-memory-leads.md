## Result in one line

This third cross-time saturation challenge does **not** qualify as low novelty. It surfaces one material governance category that is not captured adequately by generic “board/governance reform” language: the changing **Party-organization / cadre-personnel / board-management / head-office–branch authorization / KPI-and-accountability chain** inside large state-owned banks. This chain determines who appoints whom, which decisions are pre-discussed or approved at which layer, how branch executives rotate, how credit authority is delegated or centralized, how deposit/loan/profit/risk KPIs shape behavior, and how accountability changes after bad loans or cases. ## Atomic recall ledger

### A. Ownership and political-organizational governance

1. **[NEW] State ownership alone does not describe decision rights.** Large-bank governance spans state shareholder(s), Party organization, board, senior management and supervisory/control functions; their relative roles changed over time.
2. **[NEW] Party organization inside large state-owned banks is a material governance layer, not merely an external policy context.** Later-era formulations increasingly emphasize embedding Party leadership into corporate governance; exact wording/timing require Evidence.
3. **[NEW] A key point-in-time question is whether important matters were subject to Party-committee prior study/discussion before board or management decision.** Do not back-project current formulations into the 1990s/2000s without source support.
4. **[NEW] Large-bank senior executives may sit within a centrally managed cadre/personnel system.** Appointment, rotation and career incentives can therefore connect bank governance to the broader state personnel system rather than only shareholder voting.
5. **[NEW] “Central financial enterprise” / centrally managed financial institution vocabulary is a high-value search key for later Evidence, but exact taxonomy and applicable institutions vary by period.
6. **[NEW] State shareholder discipline and Party/cadre discipline can operate simultaneously but are not the same mechanism: one is ownership/capital governance, another is political/organizational personnel authority.
7. **[NEW] Listed-bank minority shareholders add another accountability channel without removing state/Party personnel influence, creating layered rather than substituted governance.
8. **[REFINE] Huijin-style shareholder governance should be separated from cadre appointment authority; the same executive can be subject to both ownership-performance and organizational-personnel evaluation.

### B. Executive rotation and career incentives

9. **[NEW] Rotation of chairmen, presidents and senior executives across large banks, regulators, other financial institutions or state roles is a material state-bank governance mechanism remembered at a high level.
10. **[NEW] Rotation can transfer practices and networks, but it can also create short tenure and horizon mismatch: executives may inherit legacy credit books and leave before long-cycle outcomes fully season.
11. **[NEW] Branch-president rotation is a separate mechanism from top-executive rotation.** It may reduce entrenched local relationships but can also intensify short-horizon growth or balance-sheet-window incentives.
12. **[NEW] Career evaluation can make nominally commercial KPIs interact with political/organizational criteria, producing behavior not captured by shareholder-value models alone.
13. **[NEW] A “successful” branch manager may be promoted based on growth/profit/asset-quality measures that contain delayed credit-cycle effects; vintage-adjusted performance can differ sharply from reported-period performance.
14. **[NEW] Accountability after later NPL emergence can reach managers who approved loans in earlier posts, raising questions of retrospective responsibility and risk aversion.
15. **[REFINE] Exact tenure rules, rotation frequency, cadre grades and appointment authorities are not asserted; they are later Evidence targets.

### C. Head office versus branch authority

16. **[NEW] The evolution from highly autonomous/local branch behavior toward stronger head-office control is a core organizational history of large state banks.
17. **[NEW] “One legal person / unified legal-person governance” or equivalent old vocabulary may be important for understanding the strengthening of head-office authority; exact contemporary phrasing must be verified.
18. **[NEW] Credit authorization is not binary centralized/decentralized: authority can be tiered by borrower, amount, industry, rating, collateral, product, branch level and risk condition.
19. **[NEW] Head office can centralize approval while branch staff retain customer acquisition and information production, creating agency problems between relationship originators and remote approvers.
20. **[NEW] Vertical risk-management lines can coexist with business-line pressure, so formal risk independence does not guarantee independence in incentives or information.
21. **[NEW] Centralized approval may reduce local capture but can produce model/checklist dependence and slower response to local SMEs or differentiated regions.
22. **[NEW] Decentralized authority may improve local information use but amplify relationship lending, local-government influence and synchronized branch growth races.
23. **[NEW] Regional/sector credit limits are an intermediate control mechanism between individual-loan approval and macro policy guidance.
24. **[REFINE] Branch-network reform should be read not only as cost/IT modernization but also as a redistribution of authority, data visibility and control rights toward head office.

### D. Credit committee, separation of marketing and approval, and risk lines

25. **[NEW] “审贷分离” (separation of loan investigation/marketing from approval) is a likely old governance vocabulary with cross-time importance.
26. **[NEW] “贷审会/授信审批/授权授信” style terms are search leads for committee and delegated-credit structures; exact bank-specific terminology is uncertain.
27. **[NEW] Committee approval can diffuse or strengthen accountability depending on whether voting records, dissent, delegated limits and post-loan responsibilities are traceable.
28. **[NEW] Post-lending management is a distinct control stage; strong origination approval cannot substitute for monitoring borrower cash flow, collateral and covenant changes.
29. **[NEW] Credit-risk verticalization may shift branch risk officers’ reporting lines toward higher-level risk functions, reducing local-business pressure but potentially weakening local integration.
30. **[NEW] Internal rating, economic-capital and RAROC/EVA systems can become governance tools rather than merely risk metrics by changing branch/product allocation incentives.
31. **[REFINE] Basel/risk-system modernization therefore has an organizational incentive dimension: models matter when tied to limits, pricing, approval and performance evaluation.

### E. KPI, budget and “scale impulse”

32. **[NEW] State-bank branch behavior historically cannot be reconstructed without KPI regimes for deposits, loans, profit, fee income, NPLs and sometimes customer/product targets.
33. **[NEW] Deposit competition could create “冲时点” / month-end, quarter-end or year-end window behavior, making point-in-time deposit balances different from average/fundamental franchise strength.
34. **[NEW] “存款立行,” “规模情结/规模冲动,” or similar old vocabulary may encode historical management culture in which balance-sheet scale was itself a competitive/status objective.
35. **[NEW] Loan-growth targets can interact with policy campaigns and branch competition, producing front-loaded origination whose asset-quality cost appears only after seasoning.
36. **[NEW] NPL-ratio targets can create denominator and recognition incentives: grow the loan book, restructure/roll exposures, delay classification or accelerate disposal to protect a headline ratio.
37. **[NEW] Provision-coverage targets can similarly affect timing of provisioning and disposal rather than only underlying risk.
38. **[NEW] Fee-income targets can encourage product migration or aggressive sales of cards, wealth, insurance and other products, creating conduct/suitability risk not visible in credit metrics.
39. **[NEW] Profit targets during margin compression can encourage asset mix, duration or credit-risk changes even when top-level strategy says “high-quality development.”
40. **[NEW] Economic-profit/EVA/RAROC adoption can counter simple scale incentives, but only if internal capital charges and transfer prices are credible and not overridden by volume/policy objectives.
41. **[REFINE] FTP is therefore also a governance mechanism: branch managers respond to internal prices, not only external loan/deposit rates.

### F. Accountability, cases and risk culture

42. **[NEW] Large-bank governance includes formal accountability for NPLs, operational losses, fraud and compliance events; the meaning and intensity of accountability changed across periods.
43. **[NEW] “尽职免责” (exemption when due diligence is fulfilled) is a later-era counterweight to excessive risk aversion, especially relevant to SME/inclusive lending; exact rollout/timing require Evidence.
44. **[NEW] Conversely, “终身责任/责任追究” style language may create long-tail behavioral effects on credit officers even if exact formal terms differ by bank/time.
45. **[NEW] Major fraud or corruption cases can function as governance regime-change signals by triggering authorization tightening, rotation, audit intensification or product restrictions.
46. **[NEW] Internal audit independence, inspection frequency and reporting line are governance variables distinct from external audit or regulatory examination.
47. **[NEW] Compliance and operational-risk functions may have moved from dispersed branch administration toward more centralized/verticalized frameworks over the long horizon.
48. **[NEW] Anti-money-laundering, sanctions and conduct controls became increasingly relevant to international and retail operations, adding non-credit constraints on growth.
49. **[REFINE] Individual cases should later be used as mechanism probes, not treated as representative of an entire bank without broader evidence.

### G. Policy mandate versus commercial/listed-bank discipline

50. **[NEW] State banks face a persistent dual-objective problem: commercially price risk and preserve capital while also responding to macro-stabilization, strategic-sector, disaster/pandemic, housing-completion, LGFV-resolution or inclusive-finance policy priorities.
51. **[NEW] The governance question is not whether policy credit exists, but who bears the performance/accountability cost when policy-aligned lending later underperforms.
52. **[NEW] Listed-bank management may frame policy support as long-term franchise or national-strategy alignment while minority investors may focus on NIM, ROE and credit cost; these are contemporaneous judgment tensions worth preserving.
53. **[NEW] State backing can lower perceived funding/credit risk while simultaneously weakening market discipline if investors assume support is unconditional.
54. **[NEW] The expectation of implicit state support can differ between parent bank, subsidiaries, products and counterparties; governance/liability boundaries matter.
55. **[REFINE] “Commercialization” should not be treated as a one-way removal of policy objectives; the long-run pattern is layered commercial discipline plus recurring policy mandates.

### H. Contemporaneous expectations later falsified or qualified

56. **[NEW] Listing was often expected to harden governance and market discipline; counter-hypothesis: formal board/disclosure reform may coexist with unchanged personnel or policy authority in important decisions.
57. **[NEW] Strategic investors were expected to transfer management/risk technology; counter-hypothesis: techniques transfer selectively while personnel/incentive architecture remains institution-specific.
58. **[NEW] Centralization was expected to eliminate local branch risk; counter-hypothesis: centralized targets and models can generate system-wide correlated errors.
59. **[NEW] Risk models were expected to reduce subjective lending; counter-hypothesis: model outputs can be overridden, gamed, or trained on benign-cycle data.
60. **[NEW] Economic-capital/RAROC systems were expected to replace scale obsession; counter-hypothesis: volume and policy targets can remain binding in practice.
61. **[NEW] Tough accountability was expected to improve asset quality; counter-hypothesis: excessive personal downside can suppress legitimate risk-taking and produce “不敢贷/惜贷” behavior.
62. **[NEW] Digitalization was expected to make branch hierarchy less important; counter-hypothesis: personnel authority, local relationship networks and offline risk work remain important for corporate/SME credit.

### J. Metric-semantic cautions

68. **[NEW] Branch “performance” may mean reported profit, economic profit, risk-adjusted return, deposit/loan growth, NPL ratio or composite score depending on era; time-series comparison needs KPI-definition provenance.
69. **[NEW] Deposit balances measured at period-end can be especially misleading under window-dressing incentives; average daily balances and pricing/cost would answer different questions.
70. **[NEW] NPL accountability based on current stock versus newly formed NPL flow versus vintage/cohort losses creates different managerial incentives.
71. **[NEW] “Approval authority” metrics should distinguish nominal limit, actual usage, exceptions and post-approval conditions.
72. **[REFINE] Organization charts alone are weak Evidence of effective control; incentive, escalation and exception behavior matter.
