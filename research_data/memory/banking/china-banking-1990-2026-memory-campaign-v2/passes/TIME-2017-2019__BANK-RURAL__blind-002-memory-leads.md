## Independent falsification leads

### A. Wholesale-fragile rural bank versus pre-existing upper-tier strategy cohort
1. The strongest second-model candidate is a rural bank whose marginal balance sheet depends on NCD/interbank liabilities and investment assets rather than inherited county deposits and loans.
2. Deleveraging and later small-bank counterparty tiering make this cohort more visible because funding-price shocks expose hidden dependence.
3. Visibility under stress does not prove a new period-wide model; the cohort existed as an upper-tier strategy before stress and remained a minority of BANK-RURAL institutions.
4. A bank can have NCD issuance without being structurally wholesale funded if local deposits still dominate total liabilities.
5. A bank can have little NCD issuance yet remain fragile because its local deposit base is concentrated, expensive or confidence sensitive.
6. Counterparty line withdrawal is a market-confidence channel distinct from retail deposit withdrawal.
7. Strong listed/market-recognized RCBs can preserve NCD access during segmentation, so wholesale funding itself is not synonymous with weakness.
8. Weak institutions may lose interbank access first, but the underlying weakness can be asset quality or governance rather than the funding structure itself.
9. The late-period external resolution shock from BANK-CITY is best treated as a market-discipline catalyst, not as a BANK-RURAL institutional event.
10. Provincial-union liquidity support can partially substitute for lost wholesale access, further preventing a clean binary between wholesale banks and deposit banks.
11. Therefore “wholesale-fragile rural bank” is a stress phenotype inside cohort heterogeneity, not a second period-wide lineage.
12. The key future metric is dependency: wholesale liabilities as a persistent share of funding plus rollover concentration and asset liquidity, not instrument presence.

### B. Shadow-asset unwind and reintermediation
13. WMP/nonstandard/委外/嵌套 contraction can make two banks with similar pre-period assets diverge sharply depending on how much hidden credit or outsourcing they used.
14. Some exposures can run off; others can return to loans; others can be converted to standardized bonds or retained through different accounting wrappers.
15. A shrinking investment-receivable balance is therefore not automatically equivalent to lower economic credit risk.
16. Reintermediation into loans can raise reported loan growth while leaving underlying borrower risk unchanged.
17. Smaller rural banks may retreat from self-managed WMP and instead distribute products from larger institutions, reducing retained risk but also fee/product differentiation.
18. Net-value transition creates operational/valuation capability demands that favor larger banks, but this is a capability gradient rather than a new bank model.
19. A simple deposit-loan RCB is a useful counterexample: it may experience little direct shadow-unwind pressure but still face the same SME/property cycle.
20. Another counterexample is a sophisticated RCB that successfully replaces nonstandard assets with liquid standardized securities without destabilizing funding.
21. “回表” should be interpreted economically: recognition may shift between loans, investments and off-balance-sheet structures rather than represent pure deleveraging.
22. The period therefore changes asset wrappers and liquidity risk more than it creates a new rural-bank lineage.

### C. Counterparty tiering, provincial unions and standalone risk
23. Market counterparties may price a member RCB’s standalone credit differently from the support implicitly expected from its provincial rural-credit network.
24. Province-level pooled liquidity or emergency support can reduce idiosyncratic funding stress while obscuring member-bank standalone liquidity weakness.
25. Provincial-union support is not equivalent to an unconditional legal guarantee; exact support mechanisms and obligations require Evidence.
26. Strong province-level coordination can stabilize weaker members but also transmit correlated policy or risk-management practices.
27. A member bank that is legally autonomous but operationally dependent on shared IT/treasury remains economically layered rather than fully standalone.
28. Counterparty tiering can differ even within one province because shareholder quality, disclosure, asset mix and market access differ.
29. A strong autonomous RCB may treat provincial-union support as backup infrastructure; a weak member may rely on it as a material liquidity backstop.
30. This continuum again argues against a binary second model.
31. Liquidity metrics should distinguish cash/high-quality liquid assets, market access, provincial-network support and deposit stability.
32. “small-bank liquidity” is therefore not one scalar state.

### D. Shareholder governance and capital replenishment
33. Shareholder pledge stress, related-party lending and hidden beneficial ownership can weaken a rural bank regardless of whether it is wholesale funded.
34. A local industrial shareholder can transmit sector downturn into both bank capital confidence and borrower asset quality.
35. Capital replenishment difficulty is often an outcome of governance/franchise weakness rather than a distinct business model.
36. Listed RCBs can have broader capital access and disclosure, yet local concentration and connected-party incentives can persist.
37. Local-government capital support can improve solvency metrics while leaving underwriting or ownership problems unresolved.
38. Repeated recapitalization is a failure-mechanism lead, not itself a lineage category.
39. Asset shrinkage can improve capital ratios mechanically, so ratio recovery after deleveraging must be separated from true capital raising.
40. A conservative bank can carry excess capital but weak profitability; a high-growth bank can be profitable yet capital hungry.
41. Shareholder exit or dilution can be part of restructuring without changing the bank’s BANK-RURAL lineage.
42. Governance quality therefore cross-cuts deposit-funded, market-funded, listed and unlisted RCBs.

### E. Recognition semantics: overdue, NPL, restructuring and write-off
43. Tighter pressure to align overdue and NPL recognition can reveal losses accumulated before the period.
44. “逾期90天以上” is a search/semantic lead, not a date-specific rule claim here.
45. A bank moving loans from special-mention or overdue into NPL can show deteriorating headline ratios while improving transparency.
46. A bank with aggressive write-offs can show falling NPL stock while credit cost remains high.
47. A bank using rollover, extension or restructuring can postpone default recognition without restoring borrower cash flow.
48. Problem assets returning from investment structures to loans can also change denominator and recognition semantics.
49. Comparing NPL ratios across banks without overdue, special-mention, write-off and transfer data can mis-rank risk.
50. Recognition discipline is therefore a governance dimension across all rural-bank cohorts, not a separate structural model.
51. Capital consumption from recognized NPLs can amplify funding stress, but the causal chain runs from asset quality through capital to liquidity rather than the reverse in every case.
52. A high reported NPL bank can be safer than a low reported NPL peer if recognition and provisioning are more conservative.

### F. Village-bank sponsor dependence and internet/digital deposits
53. Village banks remain a separate sponsor-led lineage, but their system significance is still smaller than the RCC/RCB network in this period.
54. Sponsor strength matters for systems, management, branding and crisis support; local minority shareholders matter for local governance and credit networks.
55. Sponsor-bank strategic retrenchment can create a future transfer/sale/consolidation problem even when the village bank itself is locally viable.
56. Internet deposits and platform acquisition are an emerging alternative liability channel for some smaller banks, but not yet a period-wide replacement for local deposits.
57. Platform-acquired deposits can be geographically broad and rate sensitive, weakening the traditional franchise-location link.
58. This channel can help a small bank overcome local deposit scarcity while increasing funding elasticity and customer disintermediation.
59. Internet deposit growth should be separated from NCD/interbank wholesale funding because customer type, insurance status and run mechanics differ.
60. A digital deposit strategy can exist in either a strong or weak rural bank; it is a liability technique rather than a new lineage.
61. Third-party platform dependence can create distribution concentration and regulatory-change risk.
62. Village banks can also adopt digital acquisition, so sponsor lineage and digital funding are orthogonal dimensions.

### G. Real-economy credit and shadow-unwind counterexamples
63. Deleveraging can push banks toward more on-balance-sheet SME/inclusive lending, but this can increase local concentration even while reducing shadow complexity.
64. A rural bank can become “simpler” on the balance sheet while taking more direct private-enterprise credit risk.
65. Private-enterprise support and rollover can reduce near-term defaults but can also mask weak borrowers if underwriting is policy- or relationship-driven.
66. Property and local-public-company risks can reappear through direct loans after nonstandard channels shrink.
67. A bank that exits shadow assets may still be highly exposed to the same county cycle through collateral and borrowers.
68. Therefore shadow-unwind is not equivalent to economic de-risking.
69. Counterexample: a bank with minimal shadow exposure can experience severe losses from one concentrated industrial or guarantee network.
70. Counterexample: a market-active bank can remain resilient if assets are liquid, counterparties diversified and capital strong.

71. The period reveals four dimensions of heterogeneity: funding-market dependence, shadow-asset complexity, governance/capital quality and local credit-cycle exposure.
72. These dimensions do not align cleanly into two institution classes.
73. Deposit-rich banks can be poorly governed; market-funded banks can be well governed.
74. Shadow-simple banks can have bad local loans; shadow-complex banks can hold relatively liquid standardized assets.
75. Provincial-union-dependent banks can be either strong or weak; autonomous listed RCBs can also be either conservative or aggressive.
76. Village-bank sponsor dependence is structurally distinct but remains a smaller parallel lineage already inside the BANK-RURAL ontology.
77. Internet deposits are an emerging funding technique, not yet a period-wide alternative model.
78. Late-period counterparty tiering is best interpreted as a stress test that reveals existing heterogeneity rather than creating a new rural-bank business model.
79. WMP/nonstandard unwind likewise changes asset/liability mechanics across cohorts rather than splitting the sector into a second lineage.
