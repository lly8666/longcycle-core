## Independent falsification leads

### A. Is an investment-heavy / market-funded RCB a second structural model?
1. The strongest falsification candidate is an upper-tier RCB whose marginal liabilities are interbank/NCD/market funding and whose marginal assets are bond, investment-receivable, trust/channel or other non-loan exposures.
2. This cohort can look economically closer to an aggressive city bank than to a small county RCC, especially if out-of-area financial-market assets grow faster than local loans.
3. Yet this does not by itself create a second period-wide BANK-RURAL model because legal lineage, local deposit franchise, local-government/shareholder relations and provincial rural-credit architecture can remain decisive.
4. The decisive distinction is whether market funding becomes the structural funding base for a broad rural-bank cohort or only a marginal accelerator for a minority upper tier.
5. A bank may issue NCDs while still funding the overwhelming balance sheet with household/SME/local public deposits; instrument presence is not model dominance.
6. Interbank liabilities can be tactical liquidity, seasonal balancing or asset-growth funding; only persistent dependence plus maturity transformation would indicate a different funding model.
7. Investment assets can be liquid treasury placement, government/financial bonds, or credit-like nonstandard structures; asset label alone cannot classify risk model.
8. “应收款项类投资” and similar categories need look-through because some are economically credit substitutes while others are closer to conventional investments.
9. A rural bank with a high investment share because county loan demand is weak is a counterexample to the claim that investment growth necessarily signals shadow-credit aggression.
10. A deposit-rich RCB can become a net lender in interbank markets; a deposit-poor aggressive RCB can become a net borrower. The same gross interbank balance therefore has opposite business-model meanings.
11. Upper-tier marketization is better modeled as a strategy/franchise sub-cohort within BANK-RURAL than as a second period-wide institution class unless independent evidence shows broad replication.
12. The upper-tier sub-cohort may still be important for later small-bank stress because it creates liquidity and mark-to-market transmission channels absent in traditional rural banks.

### B. Shadow-credit and bank-type boundary
13. Trust beneficiary rights, asset-management plans, wealth-management channels, entrusted structures and nonstandard credit are plausible mechanisms by which rural banks copied techniques pioneered more visibly at joint-stock/city banks.
14. Adoption of the same product technique does not erase institution-type boundaries; BANK-RURAL remains defined by lineage, governance and franchise rather than product exclusivity.
15. Off-balance-sheet WMP distribution may be customer/franchise business even when the bank does not retain material credit risk; retained risk and distribution role must be separated.
16. Investment-receivable growth should be reconciled with loans, interbank assets and WMP to avoid double counting one economic exposure across legal/accounting wrappers.
17. Local property/LGFV-like exposure held through a trust plan can leave the bank economically county-concentrated even if the accounting line appears diversified.
18. Conversely, a diversified bond book can reduce local concentration without changing the rural-bank franchise model.
19. A structural warning lead is maturity mismatch: short wholesale liabilities funding longer-duration or illiquid investments can create run/liquidity sensitivity not present in deposit-funded lending.
20. Another warning lead is reliance on repeated rollover of interbank/NCD liabilities; exact maturity ladders are Evidence-stage.
21. The absence of public WMP/NCD sophistication in a small bank is not evidence of weak risk management; simplicity can be a stabilizing counterexample.
22. Strong treasury capability may improve liquidity and earnings diversification before it creates shadow-credit risk; contemporaneous analyst judgment should not be backcast from later deleveraging outcomes.

### C. Provincial-union quasi-group architecture
23. Provincial unions can centralize core IT, payments, treasury access, product procurement, regulatory coordination and sometimes executive influence while members remain separate legal persons.
24. This layered architecture is neither pure federation nor conventional bank holding company; “quasi-group” is a useful analytical lead, not an official classification.
25. Province-wide liquidity support can reduce idiosyncratic run risk but may also weaken market discipline if member banks expect rescue or pooled support.
26. Centralized technology creates operating scale and common controls, yet common models can also propagate correlated underwriting or cyber/operational failures.
27. Personnel authority should be separated from service authority: a platform that controls appointment and strategy is materially different from one mainly providing clearing/IT.
28. Legal RCB conversion does not necessarily terminate provincial-union influence; conversion can modernize shareholder form while legacy control remains.
29. A more autonomous listed or listing-oriented RCB may pull away from provincial-union control, creating within-province heterogeneity rather than a new BANK-RURAL class.
30. Provincial-union financial statements, if any, must not be mechanically consolidated with member-bank data unless the accounting perimeter actually does so.
31. A province-level liquidity backstop can mask standalone funding fragility; standalone versus network-supported liquidity are different metrics.
32. A counterexample is a strong county RCB that can access markets independently and treats provincial-union services as infrastructure rather than substantive control.

### D. Deposit insurance, implicit support and liability competition
33. Formal deposit insurance creates a legal retail-protection floor, but household behavior can still depend on local-government reputation, provincial-union support and bank brand.
34. Deposit insurance may narrow perceived safety gaps without eliminating deposit-price competition; weaker banks may still need higher rates, gifts, relationship bundles or local public-sector access.
35. Small-bank deposits should be segmented by household, SME, public institution, village collective and corporate customer because sensitivity to formal insurance differs.
36. Large uninsured corporate/public deposits can remain highly confidence sensitive even where small retail deposits are protected.
37. A deposit-rate premium can signal growth strategy, weak franchise, maturity preference or local competition; it is not a standalone distress metric.
38. Deposit insurance can create moral-hazard concerns if management interprets retail stability as license for riskier asset growth; this is a contemporaneous judgment lead, not a proven outcome.
39. Counterexample: a conservative small RCB can pay competitive rates simply because local deposit competition is intense, not because its credit quality is weak.
40. Local-government deposit placement or payroll relationships can create sticky institutional funding that behaves differently from market-priced wholesale funding.
41. Deposit migration into WMP or internet-based products may resemble funding loss while remaining within the broader customer relationship.
42. Liability analysis should therefore separate price, tenure, customer type, insurance eligibility, institutional support and product wrapper.

### E. Digital channels versus physical franchise
43. Mobile banking and third-party payments reduce transaction dependence on branches, but branch networks can remain central for deposit trust, cash service and relationship underwriting.
44. Rural digitalization can be complementary: province-wide technology raises convenience while inherited outlets preserve customer acquisition and identity verification.
45. Digital payments may weaken fee/settlement income before weakening deposit balances; transaction disintermediation and funding disintermediation need separate timelines.
46. Third-party platforms can improve customer convenience while reducing bank-owned transaction data and daily engagement.
47. A digital app provided through provincial shared infrastructure can make a tiny bank appear technologically scaled even if its own technology governance remains thin.
48. Branch productivity ratios can improve simply because transactions move online, without equivalent improvement in credit underwriting or franchise quality.
49. Outlet closures or slower outlet growth should not be interpreted as withdrawal from rural service unless digital service and customer coverage are considered.
50. Older/rural customers, village collectives and cash-intensive small businesses may remain branch dependent long after younger users adopt mobile payments.
51. Counterexample: a dense branch franchise can still be economically valuable in low-density areas where digital adoption is uneven and relationship lending matters.
52. Operational/cyber risk becomes more systemic when many rural banks share common provincial technology platforms.

### F. Post-stimulus recognition and capital seasoning
53. 2013-2016 is a recognition window in which earlier rapid-growth loans can migrate from normal to special-mention, overdue, restructured and NPL categories.
54. The path from overdue to NPL can be delayed by rollover, refinancing, collateral restructuring, guarantee substitution or local support.
55. “逾期90天以上” versus NPL is a critical semantic gap; exact regulatory treatment and disclosure conventions must be Evidence-tested.
56. A declining NPL ratio can coexist with rising problem-loan stock if total loans grow quickly.
57. A rising NPL ratio can reflect improved recognition rather than worsening current underwriting.
58. Write-offs, transfers and recoveries change stock measures; credit cost/provision expense and NPL stock should be reconciled as flows.
59. Guarantee chains can delay recognition because one borrower’s distress is temporarily covered by guarantors until network stress becomes systemic.
60. Local-government or shareholder recapitalization can repair capital ratios without repairing origination discipline or local concentration.
61. Capital adequacy should be interpreted against RWA growth and hidden/off-balance-sheet exposures, not nominal asset growth alone.
62. Strong earnings from investment books can temporarily support capital generation while simultaneously adding liquidity/valuation risk.
63. A conservative deposit-funded RCB with visible NPL recognition can look worse on headline ratios than a more aggressive bank using restructuring or opaque investment wrappers.
64. Recognition discipline is therefore part of governance quality, not merely an accounting afterthought.

### G. Village-bank sponsor dependence and system significance
65. Village banks remain a distinct sponsor-led legal lineage but do not appear large enough in this period to replace RCC/RCB institutions as the system-wide rural model.
66. Sponsor reputation can substitute partly for inherited local franchise, especially for deposits and risk systems.
67. Sponsor-bank centralization can generate standardized underwriting and IT but can also create multiple-affiliate correlated exposure or strategic-retrenchment risk.
68. A village bank’s local minority shareholders can have incentives different from the sponsor’s regional/network strategy.
69. Cold-start funding remains structurally different from inherited RCC deposits, so village banks should be analyzed as an entrant layer even when profitable.
70. Sponsor withdrawal, sale or consolidation is a future failure/restructuring lead that could test the durability of this model.

### H. Final counterexamples and model decision
71. Wealthy coastal/peri-urban RCBs can be market funded, investment heavy, technologically sophisticated and publicly oriented while retaining rural lineage.
72. Inland agricultural RCBs can remain deposit rich, loan simple and province-platform dependent.
73. Weak industrial-county banks can combine legacy SME NPLs with costly deposits without meaningful shadow assets.
74. Village banks can be sponsor-dependent and narrowly local without provincial-union ties.
75. These four archetypes coexist, arguing for heterogeneity within BANK-RURAL rather than a binary traditional-versus-modern ontology.
76. The strongest “second model” candidate is therefore a market-funded upper-tier RCB sub-cohort, but it fails the period-wide test: it is not broad enough to define the whole 2013-2016 rural-bank regime.
77. Provincial-union architecture likewise fails the second-model test because it is a control/service layer across many legal bank forms, not a separate bank type.
78. Digital disruption fails the second-model test because it changes distribution economics across both traditional and marketized banks.
79. Deposit insurance fails the second-model test because it alters confidence architecture rather than creating a new balance-sheet model.
80. Recognition/capital changes fail the second-model test because they expose differences in risk governance rather than create a new lineage.

## Orthogonal disposition
No second period-wide BANK-RURAL structural category emerges. Market-funded/investment-heavy RCBs are a material upper-tier strategy cohort inside the broader RCC/RCB lineage; provincial unions are cross-entity control/service architecture; village banks remain a smaller sponsor-led parallel lineage; deposit-insurance, digital-channel and recognition changes are mechanisms affecting multiple cohorts.
