## Recovery thesis

The targeted gap is the **transaction franchise**: a bank's position inside the customer's operating cash-flow system. Its cross-time economics are distinct from standalone credit, deposit, payment or wealth products. Settlement primacy can generate information, balances, fee/flow revenue, cross-sell access and switching costs; lending can be used to win or defend that primacy, but large lending volume does not prove it.

## A. Point-in-time evolution of customer primacy

1. In earlier periods, branch geography, cash handling, account opening/maintenance, paper instruments and local settlement convenience likely made physical network coverage a central source of transactional primacy.
2. Corporate current accounts and settlement relationships could predate modern “transaction banking” branding; old vocabulary may include settlement, clearing, cash management, account management, agency collection/payment and key-client service rather than one stable product family.
3. As enterprise groups became more cross-regional, a single local branch relationship was increasingly insufficient; banks needed group-level coordination across branches and accounts.
4. WTO-era governance and technology investment plausibly enabled more centralized customer databases and group-account service, but the exact adoption path differed by bank.
5. Large-bank listings and stronger profitability discipline likely increased attention to fee income, low-cost deposits and wallet share beyond loan growth alone.
6. The 2009–2012 credit boom could strengthen lending relationships while simultaneously masking whether customer operating flows were truly captured; policy-driven loan expansion and transaction-franchise deepening are not the same event.
7. Interest-rate liberalization increased the economic value of sticky operating deposits and non-credit service income, plausibly making transaction banking more strategically salient.
8. Digital corporate banking and host-to-host/API-like connectivity can turn account relationships into operational infrastructure, reducing manual processes and raising switching costs.
9. Mobile and platform payments may weaken some retail payment touchpoints while enterprise cash-management integration can preserve corporate-bank primacy.
10. Low-rate/NIM pressure in the 2020s makes low-cost operational funding and fee/flow services more valuable, but also makes deposit pricing competition expose weak transaction franchises.

## B. Corporate settlement and operating-account mechanics

11. “Primary bank” should be decomposed into at least credit bank, settlement bank, payroll bank, cash-management bank, trade/FX bank, custody bank and treasury-service bank; one institution may hold several or only one role.
12. Operating accounts receive and disburse customer cash; the resulting average balances may be economically different from treasury placements deliberately optimized for yield.
13. Accounts embedded in supplier payments, collections or payroll can be sticky because changing banks requires operational reconfiguration, permissions, file formats, enterprise-system interfaces and counterparty communication.
14. Cash-management value may arise from visibility and control over multiple accounts rather than from a specific named product.
15. Group treasury functions create demand for balance concentration, intragroup payment coordination, liquidity forecasting and authorization controls; exact legally permissible structures vary by period and should not be inferred here.
16. Banks can use group-level service to prevent each subsidiary from fragmenting relationships across competing banks.
17. A corporate customer can maintain multiple banks for credit diversification while centralizing operating flows with one or two transaction banks.
18. Therefore loan syndication or multi-bank credit lines can coexist with concentrated settlement primacy.
19. Transaction banking may produce low explicit fees because the bank monetizes balances, information and cross-sell rather than pricing every service independently.
20. Relationship profitability needs a combined view of lending spread, deposit funding value, fees, treasury flow and capital/risk cost.

## C. Transaction flow → information → credit mechanism

21. Payment and collection flows can reveal sales seasonality, supplier concentration, payroll stability and liquidity stress before financial statements do.
22. Such information may improve working-capital underwriting and limit management, although actual data use depends on systems and governance.
23. A bank without settlement data may rely more heavily on collateral, external financial statements or relationship judgment.
24. Transaction data can make small-business or supply-chain lending more scalable when linked to anchor-enterprise flows, but this overlaps with `ASSET-CORPORATE-SME` and remains a boundary lead.
25. Deteriorating operating inflows or abnormal payment patterns may act as early-warning signals before formal delinquency.
26. Conversely, a borrower may route transactions away from a creditor bank, limiting informational advantage even when the loan remains on balance sheet.
27. Credit can be priced or allocated partly to win operating accounts; a low loan spread may be rational if total relationship economics are attractive.
28. The opposite failure mode is “loan first, deposits promised later”: branches may book low-return assets expecting balances or fee business that never arrives.
29. KPI design matters because separate loan, deposit and fee targets can cause branches to overstate relationship profitability or double-count cross-sell value.
30. FTP and relationship-level profitability tools are therefore part of transaction-franchise measurement, not just ALM machinery.

## D. Corporate-to-retail bridges

31. Payroll relationships create a recurring bridge from corporate coverage to retail account acquisition.
32. Salary inflows can generate demand deposits, debit-card/payment use, consumer lending eligibility and later wealth distribution opportunities.
33. The value of payroll acquisition depends on activation and retention; dormant salary accounts or immediate balance transfers provide less franchise value.
34. Employer relationships may also support employee benefits, pension-like services or other agency services; exact products and institutional rules require verification.
35. Merchant acquiring or collection services can link corporate/merchant transaction flows to retail payment ecosystems.
36. Utility, tuition, public-service or other recurring payment agency relationships historically may have increased account usage and branch traffic; exact examples should be verified rather than assumed.
37. Digital payment competition can reduce the visibility of the bank brand while the underlying deposit account remains the funding source, creating a separation between payment-interface ownership and balance-sheet funding.
38. Large state banks may retain funding advantages from payroll and settlement relationships even when fintech platforms own the customer-facing payment experience.

## E. Operational versus rate-sensitive deposits

39. Deposit categories used in public reporting do not necessarily reveal whether balances are operational; demand/term classification is an imperfect franchise proxy.
40. Some corporate demand balances can be volatile and concentrated, while some term deposits can be relationship-stable; stickiness should be measured behaviorally where possible.
41. Deposit beta under changing rates can reveal franchise quality, but beta differs by customer type, maturity and competitive environment.
42. Period-end balance management can distort snapshots; average daily balances or similar measures may better reflect operating franchise, if available.
43. A bank can “buy” deposits through pricing or structured products without controlling the customer's operating flows.
44. Conversely, an operating-flow franchise may tolerate lower posted rates because switching costs and service integration matter.
45. Risk-off episodes can generate flight-to-quality deposits at large state banks that look cheap and sticky temporarily but are not necessarily customer-franchise gains.
46. Regulatory or policy-driven liquidity changes can also alter deposit mix independently of customer economics.
47. Transaction-franchise analysis should therefore separate structural operating balances, rate-driven treasury balances, temporary safety flows and period-end managed balances.

## F. Strategic/key-account coverage and organizational frictions

48. Large state banks likely developed “key customer”, “strategic customer” or comparable coverage constructs; exact names and thresholds need Evidence.
49. National corporate groups create internal conflicts over which branch owns the customer, deposits, fee income and credit exposure.
50. Head-office coordination can reduce duplicate credit and inconsistent pricing but can also weaken local relationship-manager incentives if revenue attribution is unclear.
51. Customer-level limits and group risk views become more important when subsidiaries borrow from multiple branches.
52. Cross-selling across bank departments requires internal referral and revenue-sharing mechanisms; siloed KPIs can prevent theoretically valuable wallet-share strategies.
53. Transaction banking is therefore partly an organizational operating model, not only a technology or product suite.
54. A bank may advertise “one-stop” or “comprehensive” service while internal product silos still force the customer to manage fragmented contacts and processes.
55. Centralized data can improve customer ownership but also produce conflicts between corporate, retail, private-bank, wealth, treasury and subsidiary channels.

## G. Branch network, corporate e-banking and APIs

56. Earlier transaction franchises depended heavily on branch service quality, clearing convenience and local operations staff.
57. Corporate internet banking reduced branch dependence for routine payments while making system reliability, authorization controls and file interfaces more important.
58. Host-to-host or API connectivity, where available, can deepen integration with enterprise resource-planning and treasury systems; exact terminology and rollout dates require verification.
59. Digital integration can increase switching costs because replacement involves technical migration rather than merely moving balances.
60. At the same time, standardized payment infrastructure can lower switching costs for simpler services, so digitization is not uniformly franchise-strengthening.
61. Branch optimization may reduce transaction-service cost if digital channels absorb routine activity, but relationship-heavy or cash-intensive clients may still value physical coverage.
62. PSBC's postal/agency network remains a structural counterexample to treating all physical-channel economics alike.
63. ABC's county footprint may support different transaction relationships from BOC's cross-border/trade franchise or CCB/ICBC's corporate networks; exact comparative strength remains a lead only.

## H. Treasury, trade and cross-border flows as customer-primacy layers

64. Trade finance and FX flows can deepen transaction primacy when they arise from the same customer's import/export operating cycle; this is narrower than the full `CROSSBORDER-FX-OFFSHORE` shard.
65. Guarantees, letters of credit and settlement services can generate contingent exposure, fees and information without conventional loan growth.
66. Corporate bond-market growth can shift financing away from loans while leaving settlement, cash management, custody and treasury flows at the relationship bank.
67. Thus disintermediation of credit does not necessarily mean disintermediation of the bank-customer franchise.
68. Treasury sales can be relationship-enhancing when tied to genuine client flows, but market/trading income generated for the bank's own book should not be confused with customer-franchise revenue.

## I. Metrics, semantics and counterexamples

69. “Corporate customers”, “active customers”, “settlement customers”, “cash-management customers”, “key customers” and “transaction-banking customers” are potentially non-comparable populations across banks and years.
70. Number of accounts is not number of customers; number of customers is not active transactional relationships; active relationships are not necessarily profitable relationships.
71. Deposit share is not transaction share, and transaction volume is not necessarily monetizable franchise value.
72. Fee income can fall despite improving franchise if services are repriced to win balances or if fee regulation changes; fee growth can rise without durable franchise if market-sensitive products dominate.
73. Similar NIMs across two banks can hide different funding structures: one may rely on operating deposits, another on priced term deposits or wholesale-like corporate balances.
74. Similar corporate loan books can hide different customer primacy: one bank may control settlements and treasury flows, another may be a marginal credit provider.
75. A strong transaction franchise can be temporarily obscured by policy-driven low loan pricing because total relationship economics include funding and service value.
76. A weak transaction franchise can be obscured by rapid balance-sheet growth during credit booms.
77. Strategy slogans such as “customer-centric”, “ecosystem”, “comprehensive finance” or “transaction banking transformation” should be tested against actual operating balances, customer activity, wallet share and risk-adjusted relationship economics rather than accepted at face value.
78. The central counterexample is that **asset scale and customer-franchise scale are not the same variable**.
