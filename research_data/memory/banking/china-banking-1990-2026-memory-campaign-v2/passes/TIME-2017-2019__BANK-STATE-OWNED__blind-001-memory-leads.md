## Period frame

- 2017-2019 feels like a transition from balance-sheet expansion plus shadow/channel growth toward deleveraging, look-through, cleaner product boundaries and more explicit credit-risk recognition.
- Large state-owned banks were not simply smaller versions of aggressive joint-stock banks: their broad deposit franchises, policy roles, large corporate books, bond portfolios and capital bases changed both the pressure points and the available responses.
- The period should be read as overlapping transitions rather than one event: shadow contraction, asset-management reform, private-enterprise stress, bond defaults, small-bank/interbank stress, inclusive-finance mandates, digital transformation and late-period LPR-style pricing reform.

## Obvious landmarks and mechanisms

1. Financial deleveraging likely reduced the attractiveness of opaque channel structures and raised the value of transparent on-balance-sheet credit for large banks.
2. The tightening of look-through and asset-management rules likely forced state banks to distinguish more clearly among proprietary investment, wealth-management distribution, entrusted/channel roles and direct credit risk.
3. WMP transition was not merely balance reduction: product maturity, valuation method, implicit-guarantee expectations, customer migration and bank reputational support were separate risk dimensions.
4. Large banks' deposit-rich funding bases probably made them less dependent on interbank wholesale liabilities than some smaller peers, but they remained important providers of liquidity and counterparties to the rest of the banking system.
5. Small-bank stress in 2019 likely mattered to state banks mainly through counterparty limits, interbank market segmentation, liquidity redistribution, bond valuation, payments/settlement confidence and policy-support operations rather than classic retail deposit runs at the state banks themselves.
6. Private-enterprise financing stress became a credit-allocation issue: state banks had to reconcile de-risking from weak borrowers with policy pressure to support private and small firms.
7. Bond defaults broadened the meaning of corporate credit risk beyond traditional loan NPLs; a borrower could remain current on bank loans while bonds signaled deterioration, or vice versa.
8. Inclusive/micro finance became more explicit as a business and policy category; large banks had to build scoring, pricing, branch-accountability and digital-delivery mechanisms for smaller borrowers.
9. NIM pressure was not only about benchmark rates: deposit competition, product migration, structural deposits, wealth competition, loan mix and repricing speed all affected realized margins.
10. Digital competition increasingly shifted customer interaction from branches to mobile ecosystems, making active-user metrics, app engagement, remote account service and data-driven cross-sell relevant to old branch economics.

## Long-tail business-model leads

11. State banks may have used their large retail deposit bases as a strategic buffer during periods of wholesale-market strain, creating a relative funding advantage over smaller institutions.
12. Deposit advantage could coexist with rising deposit beta: corporate deposits, wealth-sensitive retail money and large customers may have demanded more market-linked returns.
13. Structural deposits and similar products may have functioned partly as deposit-defense tools, blurring the boundary between plain deposits and investment-like customer propositions.
14. Wealth-management migration may have produced cannibalization: customer funds moving from deposits into bank-distributed products could raise fee income while weakening low-cost funding.
15. A clean interpretation of WMP transition should separate customer AUM, bank balance-sheet exposure, guarantee expectation, underlying asset risk, fee economics and liquidity-management effects.
16. State banks could be distributors or custodians of products whose underlying credit risk sat elsewhere; distribution scale does not equal credit exposure.
17. Nonstandard credit contraction may have pushed some financing demand back onto loans or bonds rather than simply removing leverage from the economy.
18. Some borrowers previously financed through trust/channel structures may have become visible as formal bank borrowers, changing reported loan growth without equivalent new economic leverage.
19. Receivables-type investment assets and interbank investment structures should be distinguished from ordinary interbank placements and from direct loans.
20. State-bank treasury books likely gained relative importance as local-government and policy-related bonds expanded, changing duration and spread exposure even as credit looked more standardized.
21. Local-government debt migration from loans or platform credit into bonds can reduce opacity while preserving geographic/fiscal concentration and interest-rate duration risk.
22. LGFV risk in this period should not be assumed uniformly distressed; access to refinancing, local fiscal strength, land value, project cash flow and state support varied widely.
23. Property exposure needs at least four buckets: developer loans, mortgages, construction-chain credit and wealth/investment exposure to property-linked assets.
24. Mortgage books may still have appeared comparatively high quality in this period even while concentration and future-cycle sensitivity were accumulating.
25. CCB likely retained greater housing/mortgage sensitivity than peers; BOC retained more cross-border/FX sensitivity; ABC more county/rural mission exposure; ICBC more scale and broad corporate concentration.
26. Large state banks could simultaneously support SOE restructuring and discipline weak SOEs; creditor coordination, maturity extension, debt swaps and new credit were distinct interventions.
27. Debt-to-equity swap structures may have shifted some bank credit risk into investment/equity-like exposures, raising questions about valuation, control rights and exit rather than eliminating risk.
28. "Zombie enterprise" cleanup likely generated a mix of recognized NPLs, restructurings, write-offs, AMCs and recoveries rather than a single disposal channel.
29. Private-enterprise guarantee chains remained a hidden amplifier: one default could affect otherwise solvent firms through mutual guarantees.
30. Supply-side capacity reduction in steel/coal and related sectors may have improved some surviving borrowers after an initial recognition wave, giving an important counterexample to a one-way deterioration story.

## Asset quality, capital and metric semantics

31. Falling or stable NPL ratios can coexist with high NPL formation if write-offs/disposals and loan growth offset inflows.
32. NPL stock, NPL ratio, overdue loans, special-mention loans, restructured loans, credit cost and write-off volume answer different questions.
33. Provision coverage can improve because provisions rise, NPL recognition changes, disposals alter the denominator, or all three; it is not a direct measure of economic loss alone.
34. Reserve-to-loan ratio is different from provision coverage and can give a different view when NPL recognition changes.
35. Credit cost is a flow through the income statement and should not be equated with NPL ratio or write-off ratio.
36. IFRS 9 / expected-credit-loss adoption around this period may have changed staging and provisioning semantics; exact implementation timing by bank should be source-verified later.
37. Stage 1/2/3 or ECL concepts should not be back-projected casually before actual adoption; however the transition likely changed how early deterioration was provisioned.
38. Capital adequacy can improve through retained earnings, slower RWA growth, asset-mix shifts, issuance or risk-weight changes; raw capital-ratio direction is not enough to infer risk appetite.
39. Large state banks' ability to issue preference shares, subordinated instruments or other capital securities likely differed by timing and bank; exact instruments belong to Evidence later.
40. RWA optimization may have become more valuable as capital constraints tightened, favoring mortgages, sovereign/local-government bonds or lower-risk corporate exposures relative to high-RWA assets.
41. Inclusive and micro loans could raise reported high-yield asset share but also require different expected-loss and operating-cost assumptions.
42. Rapid growth in inclusive lending can mechanically reduce legacy NPL ratios if denominator expansion is strong; vintage seasoning remains necessary.

## Pricing, deposits and profitability

43. The period's loan pricing transition should distinguish benchmark-linked pricing, negotiated spreads, late-period LPR referencing and internal FTP.
44. LPR reform near the end of the period likely mattered first through new-loan pricing expectations and competitive behavior rather than instant repricing of the entire back book.
45. Deposit pricing remained constrained by customer behavior even after formal rate liberalization; large banks' franchise advantage may have shown up as lower average funding cost rather than absence of competition.
46. NIM can fall even when net interest income rises if balance-sheet growth offsets lower spread.
47. Net interest income growth, NIM, average asset yield and average funding cost should be kept separate.
48. Fee-income weakness could reflect WMP reform, card/payment competition, regulatory fee reduction or customer-mix change; it should not be attributed to one channel automatically.
49. Cost-to-income ratios may improve via digital migration and scale even while technology spending rises; denominator effects matter.
50. Branch closures are not the only digital-efficiency signal: branch role redesign, smart terminals, remote service and centralized operations may matter more than raw counts.

## Interbank, liquidity and small-bank stress

51. Large state banks may have become net liquidity providers during episodes of smaller-bank stress, reinforcing their systemic role while increasing counterparty-selection responsibilities.
52. Counterparty segmentation means "interbank rates" may not capture the funding cost of weak institutions; credit spreads and collateral eligibility matter.
53. A small-bank event can tighten money markets without implying solvency problems at large banks; liquidity, credit and confidence channels should be separated.
54. State banks' bond holdings of financial institutions create another transmission route distinct from unsecured interbank lending.
55. Repo exposure should be separated from credit exposure because collateral and haircut dynamics can dominate.
56. Negotiable certificates of deposit mattered more for smaller/wholesale-funded banks, but large banks could be buyers, market makers or benchmark competitors rather than dependent issuers.
57. Liquidity coverage and stable-funding metrics likely pushed balance-sheet structure toward more explicit liquidity management; exact thresholds/timing are not recalled safely.

## Digital, payments and customer interface

58. Mobile-payment platforms weakened banks' monopoly over daily customer interaction even if deposits legally remained in banks.
59. Third-party money-market products could pull idle balances away from demand deposits, raising the strategic value of bank wealth and mobile propositions.
60. State banks' digital response likely included mobile-banking upgrades, QR/payment integration, API/open-banking experiments, online lending and ecosystem partnerships.
61. Some bank-owned e-commerce/platform experiments may have underperformed because banks lacked consumer traffic and merchant-network advantages; these are useful failed-strategy leads.
62. Digital small-business lending reduced marginal acquisition/servicing cost but introduced model-risk, data-quality and fraud-control dependencies.
63. Branch staff incentives had to shift from transaction processing toward sales/advice and relationship management; this transition can create conduct and mis-selling risks.
64. Remote account/service growth likely increased cyber, authentication, fraud and operational-resilience importance.

## Cross-border and bank heterogeneity

65. BOC's overseas network and trade-finance franchise likely made it more exposed to RMB exchange-rate moves, global trade softness and foreign regulatory/counterparty conditions than ABC.
66. Trade tensions late in the period could affect BOC and export-oriented corporate books through client cash flow even without direct foreign-market losses.
67. ICBC's scale likely made aggregate policy priorities more visible in absolute credit allocation while diversification reduced single-business dependence.
68. CCB's housing and infrastructure franchises likely shaped mortgage, developer and local-government exposure differently from BOC.
69. ABC's county/rural network may have made inclusive-finance targets operationally less alien than for some peers but still exposed it to agriculture, county SMEs and lower-income customer economics.
70. Relative NPL ratios across the big four should not be read without portfolio mix, write-off intensity and recognition policy.

## Contemporaneous judgment leads

77. A plausible contemporaneous bull case was that stronger state banks would gain share as shadow channels shrank because they had cheap deposits, capital, compliance infrastructure and direct lending capacity.
78. A plausible bear case was that moving credit back on balance sheet would expose previously hidden risks and compress profitability through higher capital/provision requirements.
79. Another bull case was that supply-side reform and disposal of legacy NPLs would mark the peak of corporate asset-quality stress before a cleaner cycle.
80. Another bear case was that private-enterprise stress and policy-directed support would create a new generation of lower-return or politically difficult credits.
81. Digital transformation was often framed as cost-saving and customer-acquisition upside; a counterview is that it commoditized transactions and forced banks to spend heavily merely to defend the customer interface.
82. The 2019 small-bank/interbank episode could be read as evidence that large state-bank funding franchises were a systemic asset, but also as evidence that they might be required to absorb liquidity/intermediation burdens.

## Counterexamples and negative space

83. Shadow contraction did not mean all off-balance finance disappeared; forms, sponsors and risk locations could change.
84. Large-bank WMP reform did not necessarily imply large direct credit losses because many products were agency/distribution businesses; role decomposition is essential.
85. Private-enterprise support did not mean indiscriminate easing: stronger borrowers could gain access while weak firms still defaulted.
86. Bond default growth did not automatically imply bank-loan NPL spikes because repayment hierarchy, collateral and restructurings differed.
87. Higher write-offs can signal worse underlying credit, more aggressive cleanup, or both.
88. Lower NPL ratios can coexist with rising expected-loss provisioning under ECL concepts.
89. Deposit-rich banks can still face liquidity-management pressure through payment flows, collateral calls or system-support roles.
90. Digital customer growth does not prove lower cost-to-income if IT, marketing and risk-control costs rise simultaneously.
91. Inclusive-finance growth may be commercially viable with digital underwriting and low acquisition cost; it should not be assumed purely policy-loss-making.
92. Local-government bonds may lower credit opacity and regulatory capital consumption while increasing duration exposure and preserving fiscal concentration.
