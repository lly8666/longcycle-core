## Cross-time perimeter reconstruction

### 1. Bank versus group

1. **[NEW] “Bank” must be treated as at least three possible reporting objects:** parent/licensed bank, consolidated banking group, and wider state-controlled financial constellation. Historical narratives often slide between them.
2. **[NEW] Group consolidation can expand even when the branch banking franchise is unchanged.** Acquisition/creation of subsidiaries can move assets, revenue, employees and risk into consolidated statements without equivalent change in the licensed bank.
3. **[NEW] Conversely, disposal, deconsolidation or JV reclassification can shrink reported group metrics without an equivalent exit from customer relationships or distribution.
4. **[NEW] The most useful historical unit is therefore a point-in-time perimeter map attached to every major metric series.

### 2. Overseas branches, subsidiaries and investment-banking platforms

5. **[NEW] Large banks used both overseas branches and separately incorporated overseas subsidiaries.** Branches remain part of the bank legal entity; subsidiaries introduce local capital/governance/resolution boundaries.
6. **[NEW] BOC is the strongest memory counterexample to a purely domestic state-bank model because its Hong Kong/international franchise and investment-banking perimeter had unusually deep historical roots.
7. **[NEW] A BOC-Hong Kong lineage appears to involve consolidation/reorganization of Hong Kong banking operations into a separately governed listed platform; exact legal sequence and dates are Evidence questions.
8. **[NEW] Offshore securities/investment-banking platforms offered a route to securities/advisory activities that domestic commercial-bank separation rules constrained.
9. **[NEW] ICBC/CCB/ABC/BoCom later developed their own international or securities/advisory subsidiaries/platforms, but the sequencing and scope were heterogeneous and should not be assumed symmetric.
10. **[NEW] Offshore subsidiaries can fund locally, book market/investment positions and face local liquidity/capital rules, so “international assets” and “overseas profit” require legal-entity context.
11. **[REFINE] Cross-border trade finance and FX business can sit in ordinary bank branches even when investment-banking activity sits in subsidiaries; internationalization is not one perimeter.

### 3. Fund-management, insurance and other product-manufacturing entities

12. **[NEW] Mid-reform-era large banks increasingly participated in fund-management companies, often with JV/partner structures rather than simple wholly owned subsidiaries.
13. **[NEW] Fund-management affiliation separates product manufacturing from bank distribution and creates two fee pools: management fees in the fund company and distribution/custody fees in the bank.
14. **[NEW] Minority/JV governance means brand affiliation does not prove full control or consolidation; historical ownership stakes and accounting treatment must be reconstructed claim by claim.
15. **[NEW] Insurance participation similarly changes whether bancassurance is manufacturing income, distribution income, equity-accounted earnings or only third-party sales.
16. **[REFINE] “Comprehensive financial services” can therefore mean ownership of manufacturers, strategic/JV stakes, or merely cross-selling arrangements; the phrase is not a stable legal description.

### 4. Financial leasing

17. **[NEW] Financial-leasing subsidiaries were an important licensed diversification route for large banks during the 2000s/2010s expansion era.
18. **[NEW] Leasing changes asset semantics: lease receivables, residual-value exposure and equipment/project concentration are economically credit-like but not identical to ordinary corporate loans.
19. **[NEW] Leasing can magnify cyclical exposure to aviation, shipping, infrastructure, manufacturing or other capital-intensive sectors even when parent-bank loan growth appears moderated.
20. **[NEW] Parent-bank funding/support and consolidated capital consumption make leasing a group ALM question as well as a product-diversification question.
21. **[REFINE] Leasing impairment and delinquency metrics should not be inserted mechanically into bank-loan NPL time series.

### 5. Debt-to-equity / financial-asset-investment entities

22. **[NEW] The market-oriented debt-to-equity-swap phase produced specialized bank-affiliated financial-asset-investment entities as a distinct legal mechanism for restructuring distressed corporate debt.
23. **[NEW] These entities change exposure form from loan/credit to equity or quasi-equity investment, creating longer duration, valuation and exit-risk questions.
24. **[NEW] “NPL disposal” can therefore improve bank-loan metrics while the group retains economic exposure in another asset class or legal entity.
25. **[NEW] The mechanism also changes incentive alignment: the investment entity needs enterprise restructuring/upside participation rather than only debt collection.
26. **[NEW] A failed or weak debt-to-equity case may not appear as a bank NPL relapse; it may appear as investment impairment, delayed exit or low return.
27. **[REFINE] Exact regulatory capital treatment, consolidation, ownership and funding of each financial-asset-investment entity require Evidence and should not be inferred from the common mechanism.

### 6. Wealth-management subsidiaries

28. **[NEW] Wealth-management subsidiaries represent one of the clearest modern legal-perimeter breaks in the state-bank business model.
29. **[NEW] Before legal-person separation, wealth products could be originated/managed in bank departments while remaining off balance sheet; after separation, product manufacturing increasingly sits in a separately governed subsidiary.
30. **[NEW] Customer distribution can remain at parent-bank branches/mobile channels, so separation of legal risk does not imply separation of customer relationship.
31. **[NEW] Wealth AUM therefore belongs to a customer-asset/manufacturing perimeter, not the bank balance sheet; bank assets and group/customer AUM must not be summed or compared casually.
32. **[NEW] Revenue also splits: subsidiary management fees, bank distribution fees, custody fees and possible investment income are different lines.
33. **[NEW] “Breaking implicit guarantees” is partly a legal/product rule and partly a reputational-behavior question. Parent-brand support expectations can survive formal separation.
34. **[NEW] Wealth subsidiaries create a mark-to-market/net-value operating model different from accrual-style bank lending, changing earnings volatility and product-management capability requirements.
35. **[REFINE] Wealth subsidiary assets are not automatically parent-bank credit exposure even when the bank supplies distribution, custody or seed capital.

### 7. Fintech subsidiaries and internal technology boundaries

36. **[NEW] Large-bank fintech subsidiaries emerged as a later organizational response to digital competition and legacy-bank HR/procurement constraints.
37. **[NEW] A fintech subsidiary can centralize software, data, AI, cloud, cybersecurity or product-engineering talent while the regulated bank retains core customer/risk ownership.
38. **[NEW] Some fintech subsidiaries may aim to sell technology externally; others may remain quasi-captive internal service providers. External revenue is therefore an empirical outcome, not an assumed strategic success.
39. **[NEW] Technology cost can move between bank operating expense, group subsidiary expense, capitalized software/intangibles and intercompany service charges, creating cost-income time-series breaks.
40. **[NEW] Data access and model governance become parent-subsidiary control questions when analytics capability is organizationally separated from regulated risk-taking.
41. **[REFINE] Digital-channel growth should be distinguished from fintech-subsidiary creation; a bank can digitize heavily without moving capability outside the bank legal entity.

### 8. Internal specialized units that are not subsidiaries

42. **[NEW] Credit-card centers, treasury centers, private-banking units and similar specialized organizations may be managerially ring-fenced without separate legal personality.
43. **[NEW] This creates a second perimeter dimension: legal perimeter versus managerial/segment perimeter.
44. **[NEW] Segment disclosure changes can therefore mimic organizational change even when legal entities are unchanged.
45. **[REFINE] A card center’s standalone P&L or risk-management framework should not be treated as evidence of a separate legal subsidiary.

### 9. Parent-subsidiary governance, capital and internal transactions

46. **[NEW] The parent bank/group allocates scarce capital among core lending, overseas entities, leasing, asset investment, wealth, fintech and other subsidiaries; this is an implicit portfolio-choice mechanism.
47. **[NEW] Subsidiary dividend upstreaming and parent capital injections affect parent versus consolidated ROE differently.
48. **[NEW] Minority interests can mechanically affect group equity/profit attribution and should be separated from ordinary retained earnings.
49. **[NEW] Internal funding, guarantees, service agreements and asset transfers can transmit liquidity/credit/reputational risk across legal entities even where external disclosure treats them separately.
50. **[NEW] Related-party/internal-transaction rules therefore matter to group risk containment, not only to owner-connected lending.
51. **[NEW] Group audit, compliance, model validation, AML and data governance must extend across subsidiaries with different regulators and accounting categories.
52. **[NEW] Rapid perimeter expansion can outrun control infrastructure; diversification may add operational/governance risk before producing promised earnings diversification.
53. **[REFINE] Parent-brand reputational support is economically important even when there is no contractual guarantee.

### 10. Cross-selling and business-model economics

54. **[NEW] Group subsidiaries turn a loan relationship into a potential multi-product relationship: lending, leasing, advisory/underwriting, custody, funds, insurance, wealth and investment services.
55. **[NEW] The relevant profitability metric may be customer-level/group-wide contribution rather than loan spread or bank-segment ROE alone.
56. **[NEW] Cross-selling synergies can fail when referral credits, data ownership, subsidiary minority partners, customer ownership and transfer pricing remain siloed.
57. **[NEW] Product breadth can also concentrate rather than diversify risk when multiple subsidiaries ultimately finance the same property/SOE/local-government/capital-expenditure cycle.
58. **[REFINE] “Fee-income diversification” should be decomposed into true external fee pools versus revenue shifted to/from consolidated subsidiaries.

### 11. Metric-semantic breaks caused by perimeter

59. **[NEW] Assets:** parent-bank assets, consolidated assets, customer AUM and managed/off-balance assets require separate denominators.
60. **[NEW] Revenue:** bank interest/fee revenue differs from consolidated subsidiary revenue and equity-accounted investee income.
61. **[NEW] ROE:** parent-only and consolidated equity/profit can diverge because of subsidiary capital and minority interests.
62. **[NEW] Capital/RWA:** investments in financial subsidiaries, deductions, consolidated RWAs and minority capital instruments create level-specific ratios.
63. **[NEW] Asset quality:** bank loan NPLs do not capture all leasing/investment/wealth/insurance impairment or valuation risk.
64. **[NEW] Cost-income:** technology, distribution and product-manufacturing costs can migrate across entities.
65. **[NEW] Headcount/outlets:** bank employees/branches omit subsidiary staff and, for PSBC, may not capture agency-network economics.
66. **[NEW] Internationalization:** overseas branch assets, subsidiary assets and offshore customer AUM are different measures.
67. **[REFINE] Any long-cycle chart should attach a legal/reporting-perimeter note before treating apparent structural breaks as economics.

## Failed mechanisms / counterfactual tests

68. **[REFINE] Universal/comprehensive-finance ambitions may have promised diversification but sometimes reproduced the same macro-credit beta through different legal wrappers.
69. **[REFINE] JV/strategic-partner structures may have promised capability transfer but delivered uneven integration because governance and incentives remained shared or siloed.
70. **[REFINE] Wealth legal separation may clarify formal liability but not fully remove reputational support expectations.
71. **[REFINE] Fintech subsidiaries may solve talent/procurement constraints but can add duplicated systems, internal pricing disputes and data-governance friction.
72. **[REFINE] Offshore subsidiaries may diversify geography while introducing local funding, FX, compliance and resolution risks.
