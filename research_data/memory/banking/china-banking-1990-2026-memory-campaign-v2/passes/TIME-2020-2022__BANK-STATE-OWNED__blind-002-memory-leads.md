## Relief, cure and recognition: separate the state transitions

1. A one-off payment deferral for a viable borrower is economically different from repeated maturity extension; repeat count and post-relief cash generation are useful indicators of whether relief is curing or masking stress.
2. Contractual modification can reset payment schedules without proving that credit risk has normalized; modified terms, days-past-due, significant-increase-in-credit-risk judgment and regulatory NPL status need separate tracking.
3. A borrower can remain Stage 2 after payments resume if forward-looking risk is still elevated; "current" does not automatically equal Stage 1.
4. Conversely, a borrower can cure from Stage 2 if the shock was genuinely temporary and cash flow recovers, so elevated Stage 2 during the pandemic need not all migrate to Stage 3.
5. Management overlays are a bridge between model output and extraordinary macro judgment; later overlay release can mechanically improve credit cost/profit without equivalent cash recovery in that same period.
6. Overlay build, model recalibration, migration and write-off should be separated when explaining changes in allowance.
7. Relief can suppress observed arrears by changing contractual due dates, making historical delinquency comparisons less clean.
8. A useful cohort split is pre-pandemic borrowers, emergency-relief borrowers, reopening-originated borrowers and repeated-lockdown borrowers; aggregate NPL ratios mix different seasoning clocks.
9. SME cure rates after relief are a more informative later-outcome question than simply counting deferred-loan balances.
10. Strong SOE or infrastructure borrowers receiving low-price support are a counterexample to treating all pandemic policy credit as weak-credit forbearance.
11. Genuine restructuring can increase recovery value through viable maturity adjustment; maturity extension alone is not proof of evergreening.
12. Repeated refinancing with no operating cash flow is the opposite counterexample and can delay ultimate loss recognition.

## Property: group, project, household and construction chain are distinct obligors

13. Developer-group liquidity risk should be separated from a project SPV's asset/cash-flow quality; a parent can be distressed while individual projects remain economically finishable.
14. Project-completion financing can be rational new money if ring-fenced to construction and presale delivery even when legacy group creditors are impaired.
15. A bank may support completion while restructuring or writing down unsecured parent-company claims, so "supporting a developer" is too coarse a category.
16. Presale escrow/control accounts create a cash-governance channel distinct from conventional collateral; missing or diverted project cash can be as important as land value.
17. Mortgage willingness-to-pay can deteriorate because an unfinished home is not delivered even when household income remains sufficient.
18. Mortgage ability-to-pay can deteriorate from unemployment/income shock even when the home is completed; these are different loss mechanisms.
19. 2022 payment-refusal/boycott episodes should therefore be decomposed into project-delivery, borrower-solvency, legal-contract and social-coordination channels rather than read as ordinary retail delinquency.
20. "保交楼" support can improve mortgage performance indirectly by restoring expected delivery, not merely by refinancing households.
21. A completed-home mortgage in a strong city is a meaningful counterexample to broad property-sector pessimism.
22. Developer loans, developer bonds, WMP/wealth holdings, project loans, mortgages and construction-chain credit should be separately enumerated before any total property-risk judgment.
23. Construction firms and suppliers can transmit developer stress into ordinary corporate/SME books without appearing in a direct property-sector label.
24. Falling property values affect collateral recovery even if contractual borrower cash flow has not yet defaulted.
25. CCB's housing franchise is a concentration check, but expertise, customer selection and mortgage-servicing scale can offset some concentration risk.
26. BOC's property risk should be viewed alongside cross-border/FX differences; ABC's alongside county/rural exposure; ICBC's alongside scale and diversified corporate lending.

## LGFV, land finance and infrastructure feedback

27. Property weakness can hit local finances through weaker land-sale revenue, which then affects some LGFV refinancing capacity even without direct developer exposure.
28. Land-value and land-sale assumptions should not be treated as cash on hand; timing and realization matter.
29. Infrastructure support can stabilize employment and local activity while simultaneously lengthening bank asset duration and concentrating fiscal exposure.
30. Strong local governments are a counterexample: refinancing or bond migration can be orderly and economically low-risk despite the generic LGFV label.
31. Weak jurisdictions can depend more on refinancing than project cash flow, making maturity extension a key risk dimension.
32. Holding local-government bonds instead of platform loans can improve transparency/capital treatment while preserving duration and local-fiscal concentration.
33. Banks can migrate risk from loan credit spread to bond duration/yield risk rather than eliminate it.
34. Property-LGFV feedback is therefore multi-step: property sales/land revenue -> local fiscal capacity -> platform refinancing/project support -> bank loan/bond quality.

## LPR, asset repricing and deposit beta

35. Asset repricing speed differs by corporate floating-rate loans, mortgages, fixed-rate exposures and newly originated policy-favored lending; average yield cannot be inferred from one LPR move.
36. Back-book mortgage repricing may occur on contractual reset dates rather than instantly, producing lagged NIM effects.
37. Corporate pricing may respond faster through competitive renewals and new lending, so loan-mix changes matter as much as benchmark change.
38. Household precautionary savings can temporarily lower funding pressure through deposit inflows even while banks face asset-yield compression.
39. Deposit abundance does not guarantee cheap funding if balances migrate from demand deposits toward time deposits.
40. Demand-to-time deposit migration can reduce liquidity risk while raising average interest expense.
41. Corporate deposit beta and household deposit beta may diverge sharply; large corporates actively manage cash while households may prioritize safety/liquidity.
42. A state bank can accept lower spreads on green, manufacturing or inclusive loans if FTP gives branches credit for policy/strategic value.
43. FTP therefore mediates the tension between head-office portfolio goals and branch-level profit incentives.
44. NIM can compress while net interest income grows via asset volume; NII can stagnate even with stable NIM if balance-sheet growth slows.
45. Parent-bank deposits can rise during wealth-market volatility, creating a funding benefit at the same time wealth-fee income/AUM suffers.
46. Lower deposit competition during flight-to-safety periods is a counterexample to a monotonic funding-cost squeeze.

## Retail/card/consumer and SME cohort seasoning

47. Card and unsecured consumer losses can react quickly to income shocks because collateral buffers are weak and payment behavior is high-frequency.
48. Mortgage losses can lag because households prioritize housing payments, contracts are longer and collateral exists.
49. A temporary collapse in travel/offline spending can reduce card interchange/fee income even if credit losses are contained.
50. Consumer-loan balances can shrink because demand falls, not because a bank deliberately de-risks.
51. Digital origination during lockdown can lower acquisition friction but may make fraud/model risk more important than branch judgment.
52. SME risk should be separated by industry and reopening path: restaurants/tourism/offline services differ from exporters, logistics, health-related firms or digital businesses.
53. Supply-chain lending anchored to strong core enterprises can have different loss behavior from unsecured microcredit despite common "small business" reporting.
54. New-loan denominator growth can temporarily make inclusive-loan NPL ratios look favorable before vintages season.
55. Post-relief roll rates and cohort migration are better leading indicators than ending NPL ratios alone.

## WMP net-value drawdown, redemption and parent-bank funding

56. A net-value WMP can record mark-to-market losses without underlying credit default; duration/spread movement and credit impairment must be separated.
57. Customer redemption can force asset sales or liquidity buffers at the wealth vehicle while simultaneously returning cash to deposits at the parent bank.
58. This creates a paradoxical group effect: wealth AUM and fees fall while parent-bank deposit liquidity improves.
59. Reputational harm from visible WMP losses can affect customer retention even when legal loss absorption sits with investors.
60. Parent distribution responsibility, wealth-subsidiary portfolio management and custodian roles should remain distinct.
61. Legacy nonstandard assets can complicate NAV transition because valuation and liquidity are less straightforward than standardized bonds.
62. A bank with strong deposit franchise may weather wealth redemptions differently from a bank dependent on investment-product customer funding.
63. "破净" is a useful period-native behavioral search key because customer reaction to below-par NAV mattered independently of contractual credit risk.
64. A recovery after bond-market volatility is a counterexample to treating temporary NAV losses as permanent credit loss.

## Digital operations, fraud, cyber and conduct

65. Remote-service continuity requires authentication, fraud monitoring, call-center capacity, data-center resilience and cyber controls, not only mobile-app availability.
66. Faster online onboarding can create mule-account, identity-fraud and transaction-monitoring risks if control friction is reduced too aggressively.
67. Digital RMB participation can strengthen state-bank wallet/service roles while also intensifying competition over customer interface; system design itself is outside this shard.
68. Third-party platforms can originate customer traffic while the bank supplies regulated credit/accounts, shifting economics from interface ownership toward balance-sheet/API provision.
69. Pandemic digital adoption can permanently reduce transaction visits but not necessarily advisory/corporate/wealth branch roles.
70. Fraud-loss and cyber-resilience metrics are a missing dimension if digital transformation is assessed only through active users or cost-income ratios.
71. Conduct risk rises when complex products are sold remotely to retail clients without adequate suitability/explanation controls.

## BOC 原油宝 as a non-credit failure mechanism

72. The 原油宝 episode should be decomposed into underlying commodity/futures market shock, product design, rollover/settlement rules, margin/liquidation mechanics, customer suitability, operational execution, contract interpretation and dispute/reputational consequences.
73. The existence of an extreme or negative underlying market price is not itself a bank credit event; the bank's exposure arises through product structure and client arrangement.
74. Customer losses, bank settlement exposure and later remediation/settlement are separate quantities and should not be conflated without source evidence.
75. A key research question is whether product design contemplated extreme settlement conditions and how margin/liquidation processes behaved; exact mechanics are intentionally not asserted here.
76. Another question is whether retail clients understood that a bank-branded product could transmit derivatives-market losses beyond intuitive commodity-price expectations.
77. 原油宝 is a strong counterexample to bank analysis centered only on NPLs: market, model/product, operational, legal, conduct and reputational risks can dominate a major incident.
78. BOC's cross-border/markets franchise plausibly created unique product capabilities and risk surfaces compared with ABC's rural franchise or CCB's housing franchise.

## Capital, RWA, loss absorption and policy credit

79. Front-loaded provisions reduce retained earnings and therefore internal capital generation even before charge-offs occur.
80. Overlay release can improve earnings/capital generation later, but should not be interpreted as new capital injection.
81. Asset growth in low-RWA government-related bonds can support balance-sheet expansion with different capital intensity than corporate lending.
82. Mortgage, sovereign/local-government bond, SME/corporate and market exposures have different RWA economics; portfolio mix matters for capital ratios.
83. TLAC-style loss-absorbing-capacity needs for major systemic banks are distinct from ordinary regulatory capital ratios; exact institution coverage and timing require later Evidence.
84. Capital issuance, retained earnings and dividend policy are separate responses to capital pressure.
85. Policy-favored green/manufacturing/inclusive lending can lower spreads but may also diversify credit away from property/legacy industries.
86. Green/manufacturing labels do not remove borrower/project risk; policy priority is not equivalent to low expected loss.

92. Low reported mortgage NPL does not disprove unfinished-project risk; willingness-to-pay stress can be emerging before formal classification.
93. High provision coverage does not prove all pandemic/property risk is recognized; Stage 2, overlays and restructuring still matter.
94. Rising Stage 2 does not prove eventual default; cure is a valid outcome.
95. Falling wealth AUM does not necessarily mean group liquidity deterioration because funds may migrate into deposits.
96. Higher deposits do not necessarily improve margin if they term out at higher rates.
97. Infrastructure/LGFV support can be both macro-stabilizing and a source of duration/fiscal concentration.
98. Property completion finance can reduce mortgage losses even if developer parent creditors realize losses.
