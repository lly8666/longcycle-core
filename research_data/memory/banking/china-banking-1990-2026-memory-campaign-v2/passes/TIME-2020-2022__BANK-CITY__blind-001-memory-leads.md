## Period frame

- The 2020–2022 city-bank story is likely not one homogeneous “pandemic shock.” It combines an initial policy-supported credit/forbearance phase with later property, local-fiscal, consumer, and margin stress.
- A useful analytical split is support-period reported resilience versus later economic-risk realization; reported NPL may lag borrower cash-flow deterioration because extensions, renewals, grace periods, restructurings, and collateral value changes alter timing.
- City banks likely show unusually large regional heterogeneity because local pandemic intensity, industrial structure, property-market dependence, fiscal capacity, and municipal shareholder strength differ materially.
- The strongest banks may have treated the period as an opportunity to deepen local deposits, digital channels, and high-quality SME relationships, while weak banks may have experienced a governance/capital/liquidity repair cycle.

## Pandemic credit support and repayment relief

- Recall lead: temporary principal/interest deferral, renewal without repayment, re-lending, and other relief mechanisms likely matter for asset-quality semantics more than headline new-NPL formation alone.
- Relief-supported borrowers should be separated into viable-but-illiquid SMEs, structurally impaired firms, consumer-service businesses, exporters/manufacturers, and property-chain borrowers.
- “No overdue” or “not yet NPL” during relief windows is not equivalent to restored debt-service capacity.
- Renewal frequency, repeated extension, interest capitalization, collateral revaluation, and guarantor weakening are likely useful Evidence-stage keys.
- A city bank with a locally concentrated SME book could appear stable if policy support postponed migration, yet still accumulate a later-stage vintage problem.
- Counterexample lead: some city banks in resilient manufacturing/export regions may have experienced strong borrower cash flow and deposit inflows rather than broad deterioration.
- Contemporaneous-judgment lead: management discussion may have emphasized “supporting the real economy” and “stable asset quality”; later Outcome should test whether this was genuine resilience, policy-timing effect, or both.

## Property developers, mortgages, land finance, and local fiscal feedback

- The property downturn likely changed city-bank risk through several distinct channels: developer loans, project financing, construction-company receivables, mortgage exposure, land-related collateral, local SOE/property entities, and LGFV cash flow linked to land revenue.
- Direct developer credit is likely only part of the risk; trust/asset-management legacy exposures, investment products, guarantees, supply-chain financing, and related local enterprises may carry adjacent risk.
- Local concentration makes the same national property shock highly heterogeneous: city-bank exposure depends on its home-city property cycle and municipal fiscal dependence on land.
- Mortgage risk should not be treated as identical to developer risk; prepayment, sales slowdown, unfinished-project concerns, borrower income, and collateral price expectations affect them differently.
- A period-specific lead is the tension between mortgage repricing/lower benchmark-linked yields and relatively sticky or termized deposit costs.
- Local-government support for developers or projects may reduce near-term default but increase bank exposure to quasi-fiscal restructuring or extend duration.
- Land-price/collateral valuation can obscure economic loss if book values remain stable while realization value weakens.
- Counterexample lead: strong-city banks in economically diversified regions may have property exposure but enough deposit franchise and fiscal support to avoid a generalized funding shock.

## LGFV, municipal support, and local fiscal pressure

- City banks are likely more sensitive than national banks to the distinction between formal LGFV credit risk and broader municipal relationship pressure.
- Pandemic spending plus weaker land-related revenue may have increased local refinancing dependence even before later debt-resolution programs.
- Useful mechanism lead: weaker fiscal cash flow -> greater rollover/refinancing need -> bank duration extension -> lower near-term default recognition but higher balance-sheet lock-up.
- Local-government shareholder/support capacity is not binary; wealthier municipalities, poorer regions, and municipalities with many competing local financial institutions may behave differently.
- “Government-linked” borrower should be split into explicit budget-backed entity, market-oriented local SOE, construction/project company, platform company, and quasi-public-service entity.
- Counterexample lead: some LGFV-linked lending may have improved in credit quality if backed by high-quality local cash flow, while private SME books worsened more sharply.

## SME, private-enterprise, retail, and consumer transitions

- SME risk likely bifurcated sharply by sector: offline services, hospitality, transport, retail, and small property-linked businesses versus manufacturing, logistics, online commerce, healthcare, or export niches.
- City-bank local-information advantage may have become more valuable when standardized scoring was less informative during abrupt lockdown/reopening cycles.
- Relationship-banking advantage can also become a weakness if local political pressure encourages repeated refinancing of impaired borrowers.
- Consumer and credit-card risk may have been hit by income volatility, but city-bank exposure varies with retail maturity; not all city banks had large unsecured retail books.
- Mortgage prepayment and consumer deleveraging may reduce asset yield even where credit loss remains low.
- Digital small-business lending accelerated, but fast origination is not equivalent to strong underwriting; cohort/vintage analysis is a useful Evidence key.
- Fraud and identity/transaction anomalies may have risen with remote onboarding and rapid digital expansion; operational-risk controls are a negative-space check.

## Deposits, termization, NIM, and liability behavior

- Deposit competition likely intensified despite abundant system liquidity because city banks still compete for stable local franchise funding.
- A useful split is retail core deposits, payroll/settlement deposits, corporate transactional deposits, municipal/public-institution deposits, negotiated large deposits, and market/wholesale liabilities.
- Deposit termization likely raises all-in funding cost even if nominal administered rates decline.
- Customers may shift from WMPs back to deposits during volatility, temporarily strengthening funding but compressing margin.
- Conversely, net-value WMP adoption and equity/fund-market periods may pull household money from deposits into AUM products.
- Deposit stability should be assessed by behavioral stickiness, not just balance at period-end.
- Strong local franchise may allow lower marginal funding cost; weaker city banks may still pay up for deposits or rely more on wholesale instruments.
- Wholesale funding segmentation after prior small-bank stress likely persisted: issuer name, shareholder support, region, and perceived governance quality matter.
- NIM pressure likely reflects both asset repricing and deposit rigidity/termization; a flat NIM story can hide changing asset/liability mix.

## WMP net-value transition and wealth-management migration

- The end-stage transition from old expected-return WMPs toward net-value products is likely material for city banks with meaningful retail wealth franchises.
- Legacy product cleanup may reduce off-balance-sheet opacity but can expose weaker investment performance or client-retention problems.
- WMP AUM decline is not automatically loss of customer relationship if funds migrate to deposits or other distributed products.
- Deposit inflow during market volatility may be partly a wealth-channel migration rather than new customer acquisition.
- Fee income, product AUM, deposit balances, and customer count should be separated in Evidence.
- City banks without strong wealth capability may benefit from deposits but lose fee opportunity; stronger ones may use local affluent-client franchises to deepen multi-product relationships.
- Old vocabulary/search-key lead: “净值化转型”, “存量整改”, “压降老产品”, “现金管理类”, “理财子/理财业务转型”, while exact institution wording remains Evidence-stage.

## Digital-channel acceleration

- Pandemic restrictions likely accelerated mobile banking, remote service, online SME origination, and digital operations even at traditionally branch-centric city banks.
- Digital transaction share can rise without branch economics improving immediately because fixed branch cost remains.
- Remote onboarding and app traffic are weak metrics unless linked to active customers, deposit balances, credit quality, cross-sell, and cost-to-serve.
- Local government/payroll/public-service ecosystem links may have become an important digital customer-acquisition channel.
- Fintech partnerships may provide rapid capability but create vendor/model-risk, data-governance, and customer-ownership questions.
- Counterexample lead: some smaller banks may have had strong local relationships but weak technology execution, widening the gap with stronger listed city banks.

## Asset-quality, provisioning, capital, and semantic breaks

- Reported NPL ratio may be mechanically affected by denominator growth, relief/restructuring treatment, write-offs, disposals, and loan migration.
- “Special mention”, overdue, restructured, forborne/extended, and NPL should be tracked as different states rather than collapsed.
- Provision coverage can rise because of denominator changes as well as reserve build; absolute allowance, credit cost, write-off, recovery, and migration rates are better paired metrics.
- Property/LGFV exposure may migrate from loans into investments or other receivables; look-through economics matter more than accounting label.
- Capital pressure may come from risk-weighted-asset growth during policy-supported lending even before loss recognition.
- Local-government or shareholder capital injections, convertible/secondary-capital instruments, and retained earnings are different forms of repair and should not be treated as equivalent.
- Weak profitability plus high provisioning can create a feedback loop where banks need external capital to continue credit support.
- Counterexample lead: high-quality city banks may have built reserves early and later shown falling credit cost, while weak banks remained in recognition/recapitalization cycles.

## Governance, ownership, and weak-bank restructuring

- Pandemic/property stress may reveal pre-existing governance weaknesses rather than create them: opaque private shareholders, related-party lending, concentrated borrowers, weak board independence, and branch-level control gaps.
- Municipal shareholder support can stabilize funding but can also reinforce policy-directed lending or delay loss recognition.
- A useful distinction is governance repair, capital repair, liquidity repair, and franchise repair; one does not guarantee the others.
- Recapitalization can restore ratios without solving asset-quality or earnings problems.
- Management replacement, shareholder restructuring, state-asset involvement, and balance-sheet cleanup may occur in different sequences across troubled city banks.
- Forgotten-actor lead: provincial/municipal state-asset entities and local investment groups may become important recapitalization actors; exact cases should be Evidence-verified.
- Counterexample lead: private-shareholder participation is not automatically weak governance, and state ownership is not automatically strong governance.

## Regional heterogeneity and strong-bank counterexamples

- Coastal/export-manufacturing city banks may have different pandemic outcomes from tourism/service-heavy or property-dependent regions.
- Strong metropolitan banks may benefit from population/income concentration, payroll deposits, tech ecosystems, and affluent wealth clients.
- Industrial-region banks may have larger manufacturing credit opportunity but greater sector concentration.
- Northeast/interior or lower-growth regions may face weaker fiscal capacity, population outflow, and property pressure, but exact geographic mapping requires Evidence.
- The period should not be summarized as universal deterioration: some banks may simultaneously gain deposits, improve digital engagement, and take market share.
- Strong-bank counterexample is essential to distinguish sector-wide macro effects from weak-bank governance effects.

## Negative-space checks

- Do not conflate city banks with rural commercial banks or village banks even when both are “small and medium banks.”
- Do not assume all weak-bank restructurings belong to the 2020–2022 slice; some antecedents predate it and some outcomes occur later.
- Do not equate pandemic relief with regulatory forbearance in a single precise legal sense without Evidence.
- Do not infer exact default timing from broad property-cycle memory.
- Do not assume all deposit growth is low-cost or all term deposits are unstable.
- Do not assume WMP runoff mechanically improves liquidity if customer funds leave the bank group.
- Do not treat local-government ownership as a guarantee.
- Do not treat low reported NPL as low economic impairment.
- Do not treat capital injection as proof of completed resolution.
- Do not treat digital growth as proof of lower credit loss or cost-income ratio.
