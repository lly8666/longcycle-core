## Failed mechanisms / contemporaneous expectations / counterexamples

61. [D] Expectation that listing alone would fully commercialize large state banks is already contradicted by the persistent coexistence of policy roles and listed-bank disciplines.
62. [R] Expectation that very large branch networks were only a cost burden was weakened by their role in deposit gathering, public-service continuity and customer access, although digital migration changed the economics.
63. [R] Opposite expectation—that branch scale guarantees franchise durability—can also fail when customers migrate digitally and operating balances become more rate-sensitive or platform-mediated.
64. [R] Expectation that rapid policy credit is automatically loss-making is too strong; borrower quality, pricing, support, refinancing and macro recovery can make outcomes heterogeneous.
65. [R] Opposite expectation—that state-supported borrowers are effectively riskless—is also too strong; economic loss can appear through restructuring, yield compression, duration extension or delayed recognition.
66. [R] Expectation that low headline NPL ratios prove superior resilience is falsifiable by later write-offs, restructuring, special-mention migration, credit cost or asset reclassification.
67. [R] Expectation that state support necessarily crowds out commercial discipline is too simple; listed-bank targets, capital constraints, internal risk systems and shareholder scrutiny can coexist with policy execution.
68. [R] Expectation that flight-to-safety deposits are permanently sticky is also too strong; balances can reverse when uncertainty fades or when pricing gaps widen.
69. [R] Apparent crisis alpha can therefore be temporary liquidity share gain rather than durable relationship economics.
70. [D] The core counterexample remains that banks of similar scale can display different resilience because of customer mix, geographic network, transaction franchise, cross-border exposure and policy mission—already covered dimensions rather than a new ontology.
