## 1. Layered authority: shareholder, Party organization, board, management

1. **[NEW] Large state-bank governance is layered rather than reducible to ownership.** State shareholder(s), Party organization, board, senior management and control functions can each hold different decision or personnel rights.
2. **[NEW] Point-in-time reconstruction must distinguish ownership authority from personnel/organizational authority.** A shareholder can exercise capital/governance rights while executive appointment and career management follow another organizational channel.
3. **[NEW] Later-era corporate-governance language increasingly places Party leadership explicitly inside governance processes; exact procedure and effective date must be verified rather than back-projected.
4. **[NEW] “党委会前置研究讨论” is a strong later search-key lead for decisions that move through a Party-organization layer before board/management action; precise scope differs by institution/time.
5. **[NEW] “三重一大” is another likely governance search key for major decisions, appointments, projects and large-value funds; its exact application to each bank requires Evidence.
6. **[NEW] Listed-bank boards and minority shareholders add market accountability but do not necessarily displace state-shareholder or personnel authority.
7. **[NEW] Board independence can therefore improve challenge/disclosure while still operating within a broader state/Party governance architecture.
8. **[REFINE] “Modern corporate governance” should be tested as an incremental layer, not assumed to have replaced prior organizational authority in one step.

## 2. Senior-executive appointment and rotation

9. **[NEW] Large-bank chairmen/presidents and other senior executives are remembered as part of a broader centrally managed financial-cadre ecosystem; exact grades and appointment authority require verification.
10. **[NEW] Executive movement among major banks, regulators, other financial institutions and state roles can transmit management practices and policy priorities across institutions.
11. **[NEW] Rotation also creates a horizon problem: incoming executives inherit legacy portfolios while reported-period performance may reflect prior management decisions.
12. **[NEW] A long-cycle outcome study should therefore attach executive tenure/vintage to major credit expansions, restructuring programs and later NPL formation rather than assign outcomes only to the manager present when losses surface.
13. **[NEW] Promotion incentives may combine commercial performance, organizational evaluation, compliance and policy-task execution; no single shareholder-return metric captures the career objective.
14. **[REFINE] Exact executive biographies are later Evidence work; this pass retains only the personnel-system mechanism.

## 3. Branch-manager appointment, rotation and local capture

15. **[NEW] Provincial/major-city branch presidents are powerful transmission nodes between head-office strategy and local governments/SOEs/corporates.
16. **[NEW] Branch-manager rotation can reduce entrenched local-client relationships and fraud/capture risk but can shorten managerial horizons.
17. **[NEW] Short tenure plus annual growth targets can encourage origination whose losses emerge after the manager has rotated.
18. **[NEW] Local relationship value and head-office standardization create a persistent trade-off: too much local autonomy raises capture/connected-credit risk; too little can destroy information advantage.
19. **[NEW] Branch-manager performance should be evaluated by vintage/cohort credit outcomes and funding economics, not only point-in-time profit/NPL ratios.
20. **[REFINE] Rotation frequency and mandatory-tenure rules are uncertain and should not be invented.

## 4. Head-office versus branch authorization

21. **[NEW] Strengthening “one legal person”/head-office authority is a major organizational trajectory of commercialized state banks, but authority remains tiered rather than fully centralized.
22. **[NEW] Authorization matrices can vary by borrower type, industry, rating, collateral, amount, product, maturity and branch level.
23. **[NEW] Head office can centralize credit approval while branches retain marketing, due diligence and post-lending monitoring, creating information handoff risk.
24. **[NEW] Regional/sector limits allow head office to constrain branch portfolios without approving every loan individually.
25. **[NEW] Emergency/policy campaigns can temporarily change effective authorization or risk tolerance even if formal matrices remain unchanged.
26. **[REFINE] “一级法人、授权经营” or similar wording is an old-vocabulary lead; exact institutional phrasing requires source verification.

## 5. Credit committees and separation of duties

27. **[NEW] `审贷分离`, `贷审会`, `授信审批`, `授权授信` are high-value historical search keys for the separation and committee structure of credit decisions.
28. **[NEW] Separation of relationship origination from approval reduces single-officer discretion but can create “information producer versus decision maker” agency problems.
29. **[NEW] Committee governance only creates accountability if voting, dissent, exceptions, delegated limits and responsibility are traceable.
30. **[NEW] Vertical risk officers/risk lines can reduce local-business pressure but may become checklist-driven or remote from local borrower information.
31. **[NEW] Post-lending management is a separate control stage: financial monitoring, covenant/collateral review, cash-flow observation and early-warning escalation can fail even when origination approval was sound.
32. **[REFINE] Internal ratings and Basel tools matter behaviorally only when linked to approval limits, pricing, capital charges and management KPIs.

## 6. Deposit, loan, profit and fee KPIs

33. **[NEW] Large-bank branch behavior historically reflects explicit budget/KPI systems for deposits, loans, profit, fee income, NPLs and customer/product targets.
34. **[NEW] Period-end deposit targets can create `冲时点` behavior, making month/quarter/year-end balances a management artifact rather than a clean franchise measure.
35. **[NEW] `存款立行`, `规模冲动/规模情结`, and possible `以贷转存` language are useful old search keys for balance-sheet-scale culture and distorted funding behavior.
36. **[NEW] Loan-growth targets can produce front-loaded credit expansion whose losses surface after seasoning; reported annual profit and NPL ratios can therefore reward risk before outcomes mature.
37. **[NEW] NPL-ratio targets create recognition/denominator incentives: accelerate loan growth, restructure, roll, dispose or delay classification.
38. **[NEW] Fee targets can push wealth, insurance, card and other product sales and introduce conduct/suitability risk outside conventional credit metrics.
39. **[NEW] Margin compression can shift incentives from scale toward asset mix, duration, fees or higher-yield credit, creating new risk even when total loan growth slows.
40. **[REFINE] Average daily deposit, marginal funding cost and customer persistence can be better franchise measures than period-end deposits when window incentives are strong.

## 7. Economic capital, RAROC, EVA and FTP

41. **[NEW] Economic-capital/RAROC/EVA systems are governance technologies: they try to replace gross scale/profit targets with capital- and risk-adjusted performance.
42. **[NEW] Their effectiveness depends on whether internal capital charges actually constrain branch/product budgets or are overridden by volume/policy objectives.
43. **[NEW] FTP converts the bank-wide funding curve into branch/product incentives; it can determine whether a branch prefers deposits, mortgages, corporate loans or bonds independently of headline external rates.
44. **[NEW] Transfer-pricing design can allocate interest-rate and liquidity risk centrally to treasury while leaving credit/customer risk with business units.
45. **[NEW] Poorly calibrated FTP can generate artificial “profitability” at branch/product level and drive volume into economically unattractive assets.
46. **[NEW] Risk-adjusted KPI adoption may reduce scale incentives but also encourage model gaming or migration toward exposures with favorable regulatory/internal weights.
47. **[REFINE] Exact dates and bank-specific formulas for RAROC/EVA/FTP are later Evidence targets.

## 8. Accountability and risk culture

48. **[NEW] NPL/accountability systems can link bad outcomes back to earlier approvers/originators, making long-tail personal downside part of credit pricing behavior.
49. **[NEW] Strong accountability can improve discipline but can also create excessive risk aversion, especially after regulatory or anti-corruption campaigns.
50. **[NEW] `尽职免责` is a useful later search key for balancing SME/inclusive-finance credit expansion with fear of personal liability.
51. **[NEW] `责任追究`, possible long-duration/lifelong accountability language, and bad-loan tolerance rules should be mapped separately because they generate different risk-taking incentives.
52. **[NEW] Fraud/corruption cases can produce organization-wide tightening of authority, rotation, audit and approval even when the case is not representative of normal business.
53. **[NEW] Internal audit independence and reporting line are central: an audit function subordinate to the business it examines has different incentive power from a vertical/head-office line.
54. **[NEW] Compliance, operational-risk, AML and sanctions controls increasingly constrain growth alongside credit risk, especially in retail and international businesses.
55. **[REFINE] Specific cases should later serve as mechanism tests only with claim-scoped evidence, not as broad character judgments about an institution.

## 9. Policy mandates versus commercial/listed-bank accountability

56. **[NEW] State banks repeatedly face dual objectives: preserve commercial returns/capital and execute macro-stabilization or national-strategy tasks.
57. **[NEW] The key governance question is who owns downside when policy-aligned credit later underperforms: branch, business line, parent bank, state shareholder, fiscal channel or another restructuring mechanism.
58. **[NEW] Policy tasks can be embedded through targets, sector priorities, pricing tolerance, capital allocation or management evaluation rather than explicit “policy loans.”
59. **[NEW] Minority/listed shareholders may evaluate NIM/ROE/credit cost while management also emphasizes strategic/national-service objectives; these contemporaneous tensions should be stored as Judgment leads.
60. **[NEW] Implicit state support can lower funding risk while also weakening external market discipline if counterparties expect rescue.
61. **[REFINE] Commercialization is therefore not a linear retreat of policy objectives; the durable pattern is layered commercial discipline plus recurring policy mandates.

## 10. Governance reform expectations and failed mechanisms

62. **[NEW] Listing was expected to harden governance; a falsifiable counter-hypothesis is that board/disclosure change can coexist with persistent personnel and policy authority.
63. **[NEW] Strategic investors were expected to transfer management capability; transfer may have been selective where incentive/personnel architecture remained unchanged.
64. **[NEW] Centralization was expected to reduce local credit risk; it can instead create correlated head-office/model errors across the whole network.
65. **[NEW] Risk models were expected to depersonalize credit decisions; models can be overridden, gamed or calibrated on benign-cycle data.
66. **[NEW] Tough accountability was expected to improve asset quality; over-deterrence can create `惜贷/不敢贷` behavior and undermine inclusive/private-sector policy goals.
67. **[NEW] Digitalization was expected to reduce branch hierarchy; corporate/SME lending can still depend on local information, personnel authority and offline monitoring.
68. **[REFINE] Governance reform should be evaluated by changed decision rights/incentives and outcomes, not by formal organization charts alone.

## Metric-semantic cautions

- Nominal delegated credit limits are not identical to actual authority after sector limits, exceptions and policy overlays.
- Organization charts show formal structure; effective decision rights require Evidence on escalation, override, exception and personnel incentives.
