## Pricing liberalization, deposits and profitability

1. **[new_category][high] Progressive rate liberalization changed large-bank pricing from benchmark-following toward customer-level economics.** The meaningful transition is not a single deregulation date but a multi-year weakening of administered loan/deposit-rate anchors, increasing the importance of borrower risk pricing, relationship value and internal funds-transfer pricing.

2. **[new_category][high] Loan pricing competition could compress spreads first on high-quality SOE/large-corporate borrowers.** Large state banks competed for the safest strategic clients, so liberalization did not automatically increase loan yields; top borrowers could gain bargaining power even as weaker SMEs paid higher spreads.

3. **[new_category][high] Deposit liberalization increased liability beta and franchise differentiation.** A huge retail/corporate deposit base remained valuable, but banks increasingly had to decide when to defend balances through rate, product bundling, wealth products or relationship services rather than relying on a uniform regulated price.

4. **[useful_refinement][high] NIM compression and net-interest-income growth could diverge.** Asset growth, mortgage expansion and balance-sheet scale could partly offset lower spreads, so slowing NIM is not identical to falling interest income or franchise loss.

5. **[new_category][high] FTP became a more important internal control variable.** When deposit scarcity and asset pricing became more market-sensitive, head office could transmit marginal funding costs to branches/business lines through transfer prices, changing which loans or deposits appeared profitable.

6. **[new_category] The early LPR-style lending reference may have coexisted with legacy benchmark habits.** Banks and borrowers could still think in benchmark discounts/premiums while newer reference mechanisms developed. Historical pricing series should not assume immediate behavioral regime change when a new reference rate appears.

7. **[new_category] Large state banks may have retained a deposit-pricing advantage because of perceived safety and transaction franchises.** Deposit insurance and formal marketization need not erase the customer perception that the largest state banks were exceptionally safe.

8. **[new_category][high] WMPs became both investment products and liability-competition instruments.** A customer could move money from deposits into bank wealth products while remaining in the same bank relationship, changing reported deposit balances, fee income and liquidity/reputational economics.

9. **[new_category][high] Internet money-market products created a visible opportunity-cost benchmark for retail deposits.** The rise of high-liquidity online cash-management products forced large banks to respond through mobile banking, wealth products, deposit pricing and user experience rather than branch presence alone.

10. **[new_category] “存款搬家” / deposit migration is a period-native vocabulary lead.** Funds could move among deposits, WMPs, money-market funds, securities accounts and other investment products; customer assets under relationship and bank balance-sheet deposits are different metrics.

## Asset-quality recognition and legacy-vintage seasoning

11. **[new_category][high] 2009–2012 credit vintages likely began to produce more visible NPL formation in this slice.** Construction periods, refinancing cycles and slower nominal growth meant risks that were latent during the boom could migrate into special-mention, overdue or NPL categories.

12. **[new_category][high] Rising NPL ratios can reflect greater recognition as well as worse economics.** A bank that classifies weak loans more promptly may report deterioration sooner than a bank that restructures or rolls exposures. Cross-bank asset-quality comparisons need recognition-policy context.

13. **[new_category][high] New NPL formation versus ending NPL balance becomes essential.** Write-offs, recoveries, disposals and upgrades can offset inflows, so the ending stock may understate the amount of credit deterioration passing through the book.

14. **[new_category][high] Provision coverage can decline mechanically when recognized NPLs grow faster than reserves.** A falling coverage ratio does not necessarily mean reserves were cut; it can result from denominator recognition catching up with latent risk.

15. **[new_category][high] Credit cost becomes a major earnings bridge.** Slower pre-provision profit growth plus higher impairment expense can explain profit deceleration even when revenue remains positive. Historical analysis should distinguish pre-provision operating profit from net profit.

16. **[new_category] Write-off/disposal intensity may rise before headline NPL ratios stabilize.** Large banks could use stronger earnings and reserve buffers to accelerate cleanup, which makes loss-recognition flow data more informative than a single ratio.

17. **[new_category] National and emerging local AMCs may provide disposal channels.** Exact counterparties and transaction forms vary, but bad-asset transfer/sale becomes a balance-sheet-management mechanism rather than only an exceptional late-1990s cleanup tool.

18. **[new_category] Credit-asset securitization can become a capital/liquidity management tool.** Large state banks may participate in revived loan securitization programs; the analytical question is whether securitization transferred meaningful risk or mainly diversified funding/capital usage.

19. **[new_category] NPL securitization re-emerges as a specialized disposal lead late in the slice.** Exact pilot dates, banks and structures are uncertain, but the mechanism is worth later source recovery as a bridge between traditional write-offs/transfers and market-based distressed-asset disposal.

## Overcapacity, SOE, private-sector and local-government risk

20. **[new_category][high] Overcapacity industries became a recognizable corporate-credit problem.** Steel, coal, shipbuilding and other heavy industries could face falling prices, excess capacity, weak cash flow and high leverage, turning previously strategic large-corporate loans into restructuring cases.

21. **[new_category][high] “两高一剩” / high-pollution, high-energy and overcapacity-sector terminology is a useful old-policy search key.** Exact definitions changed, but sector-control lists likely affected branch limits, new lending and exit strategies.

22. **[new_category][high] Zombie-SOE lending creates default-versus-extension ambiguity.** Weak SOEs may continue servicing debt through subsidies, asset sales, group support or refinancing, producing low default frequency but poor economic return and capital drag.

23. **[new_category] SOE mergers/reorganizations can transfer credit support without producing formal default.** A weak borrower absorbed into a stronger group may repay through sponsor support, making institution-level loss outcomes different from stand-alone borrower economics.

24. **[new_category][high] Supply-side capacity reduction changes bank strategy from simple rollover to selective exit/restructuring.** By the latter part of the slice, banks likely faced pressure to distinguish viable firms from “zombie” capacity and to support mergers, closures or debt restructuring.

25. **[new_category][high] Market-oriented debt-to-equity swap concepts reappear near the end of the slice.** Banks or bank-linked implementation vehicles could exchange debt claims for equity exposure under restructuring frameworks; exact legal structures and risk transfer require later Evidence.

26. **[new_category][high] Private-enterprise guarantee chains remain a correlated-risk channel.** Mutual guarantees and regional business networks can transmit one corporate failure into multiple bank borrowers, especially when collateral values and local economic conditions weaken together.

27. **[new_category] Trade-finance/collateral fraud lessons likely strengthened collateral custody and group-exposure controls.** Warehouse receipts, repeated pledges and related-party trade flows are control problems as much as sector-credit problems.

28. **[useful_refinement][high] LGFV risk shifts from pure platform-loan growth toward refinancing/reclassification and local-debt restructuring.** The research question becomes how old bank loans were refinanced, swapped, converted to bonds or kept as corporate/platform exposures.

29. **[new_category][high] Local-government bond/debt-swap programs can alter both credit risk and bank asset mix.** Replacing some platform loans with local-government bonds may reduce borrower-specific risk and improve transparency while lowering yield and extending duration; banks can move from lender to bond investor without eliminating local-fiscal exposure.

30. **[new_category] Loan-to-bond migration changes metric comparability.** A decline in LGFV loan balance does not necessarily mean the bank reduced local-government economic exposure if it simultaneously increased holdings of related government bonds.

## Property and retail transition

31. **[new_category][high] The property cycle within 2013–2016 is not monotonic.** A slowdown/adjustment phase and later policy easing/property recovery can produce very different developer and mortgage vintages. Treating the entire slice as one property regime would hide timing.

32. **[new_category][high] Mortgage growth can accelerate as banks favor relatively standardized retail assets.** Compared with overcapacity corporate loans, mortgages may offer granular borrowers, collateral and lower observed default, even if yields are lower and property concentration rises.

33. **[new_category] CCB may be especially informative for mortgage/housing-finance strategy because of its historical construction/housing franchise.** Concentration could produce both product expertise and greater sensitivity to property policy; exact shares require Evidence.

34. **[new_category] Consumer/card lending becomes a more meaningful diversification line.** Credit scoring, centralized collections and transaction data make retail risk management structurally different from corporate relationship lending.

35. **[new_category] Private banking/wealth management deepen as affluent customers shift from deposits toward investment products.** Relationship economics increasingly include AUM, custody and distribution, making deposit balances a narrower measure of customer franchise.

## Wealth, nonstandard assets and interbank structures

36. **[new_category][high] WMP portfolios likely broadened into bonds, money-market assets and nonstandard credit claims.** The critical lead is asset composition and risk-bearing entity, not merely WMP issuance volume.

37. **[new_category][high] “非标准化债权资产” / nonstandard debt claims become period-native vocabulary.** This category can include credit-like exposures not traded on standard exchanges/interbank bond markets; exact regulatory definitions must be sourced later.

38. **[new_category][high] Trust, securities-firm and fund-subsidiary channels can move credit exposure outside ordinary loans.** Banks may originate clients/funding while another vehicle holds the asset; the economic chain needs look-through mapping to avoid assuming legal form equals risk transfer.

39. **[new_category] State banks may have been less dependent on aggressive interbank-investment structures than joint-stock peers.** Their deposit franchises reduced funding pressure, but they could still act as fund providers, counterparties, product distributors or investors in the same ecosystem.

40. **[new_category] Interbank certificates of deposit and other market liabilities become relevant opportunity-cost benchmarks even for deposit-rich banks.** They can affect treasury pricing and marginal liquidity value without becoming the dominant funding source.

41. **[new_category][high] Receivables-type investments / buy-resale structures can complicate loan-versus-investment comparisons.** Credit-like assets booked outside loans may carry different provisioning, capital or disclosure semantics. The prevalence at the largest state banks should be tested rather than assumed.

42. **[new_category] Regulatory tightening can cause reclassification rather than immediate disappearance.** When one channel becomes constrained, assets may migrate among WMP, trust, securities, interbank, bond or loan forms. A time series should distinguish genuine deleveraging from accounting/legal-form migration.

## Capital, liquidity and risk architecture

43. **[new_category][high] Basel III-style capital composition becomes more salient than a single capital-adequacy ratio.** Common equity, additional Tier 1 and Tier 2 quality matter differently for loss absorption; rapid RWA growth and credit deterioration can pressure buffers even when total ratios look comfortable.

44. **[new_category] Preference-share and other capital-instrument issuance becomes a plausible large-bank replenishment mechanism.** Exact instruments and dates differ by bank; the research lead is that capital management diversified beyond retained earnings/common equity and older subordinated debt.

45. **[new_category][high] Liquidity coverage and stress-testing concepts increase the value of high-quality liquid assets.** Large deposit franchises remain an advantage, but liquidity management becomes more explicitly tied to runoff assumptions, liquid-asset buffers and market stress rather than only loan-to-deposit ratios.

46. **[new_category] Macroprudential assessment can make broad asset growth/capital quality a management constraint by the end of the slice.** This should be treated through bank behavior—asset allocation, capital planning and off-balance incentives—rather than duplicating system-rule chronology.

47. **[new_category] Advanced internal-rating/economic-capital systems now have a longer data history but face model-risk problems.** Structural changes in policy support, property and SOE behavior can make historical default data poor predictors even when models are technically mature.

## Digital channels and franchise response

48. **[new_category][high] Mobile/internet finance shifted competition from branch density toward daily customer engagement.** Third-party payment and internet platforms could own transaction frequency while banks still held deposits/settlement accounts, weakening the old assumption that account ownership equals customer-interface control.

49. **[new_category][high] Large banks responded with mobile apps, online banking and bank-owned internet/e-commerce experiments.** Some bank e-commerce platforms may later prove less durable than payment/fintech competitors; these are useful failed/dead-end strategy leads rather than only success stories.

50. **[new_category] Branch networks changed from transaction processing toward sales/advice/service.** Digital migration can improve cost efficiency but makes legacy branches and staffing a fixed-cost challenge, especially for very large networks such as ABC.

51. **[new_category] Payment-platform competition can affect low-cost deposits indirectly.** If third-party wallets/money funds capture transaction balances, banks may retain underlying settlement deposits yet lose direct customer pricing power and cross-sell visibility.

## Cross-border and four-bank heterogeneity

52. **[useful_refinement][high] BOC remains the clearest cross-border/FX counterexample.** RMB internationalization, offshore clearing, trade finance and overseas branches can support fee/market income but create exchange-rate, country and external-cycle sensitivities less central to ABC/CCB.

53. **[new_category] Belt-and-Road / overseas infrastructure financing emerges as a late-slice strategic-client channel.** For state banks this can combine policy support, SOE client relationships, cross-border credit and sovereign/country risk; exact project exposures require Evidence.

54. **[new_category] ICBC’s global acquisition/overseas expansion strategy can add non-domestic earnings and operational complexity.** The key lead is governance and integration of overseas subsidiaries, not specific deal terms.

55. **[useful_refinement] ABC remains structurally distinct because county/rural deposit and credit networks coexist with urban corporate/retail modernization.** Digital channels may lower rural service costs but also expose the bank to fintech competition without eliminating policy mission.

## Contemporaneous judgments and counterexamples

56. **[new_category][high] The management narrative likely shifted from “rapid reform and growth” toward “new normal, risk recognition and transformation.”** Slower economic growth, rising NPLs and rate liberalization made asset quality, capital, fee income, retail and efficiency more prominent than pure loan growth.

57. **[new_category][high] Counterexample: rising NPLs can be evidence of improved transparency and cleanup capacity.** A bank recognizing and disposing weak loans faster may look worse in headline ratios before becoming economically healthier.

58. **[new_category][high] Counterexample: local-debt swaps can reduce expected credit loss while hurting yield.** Safer lower-yield government bonds may replace riskier platform loans, improving asset quality but compressing interest income; “lower return” need not mean worsening credit quality.

59. **[new_category] Counterexample: state banks may be less exposed to shadow-channel leverage than industry aggregates imply.** Faster-growing joint-stock/smaller institutions may dominate some interbank/nonstandard structures; large-state-bank exposure must be measured bank by bank rather than inferred from system totals.

60. **[new_category] Counterexample: digital disruption can strengthen rather than weaken large-bank economics when technology migrates transactions to lower-cost channels.** Loss of branch traffic is not automatically franchise loss if deposits, payments, cards and mobile engagement stay within the bank.
