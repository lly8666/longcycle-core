## Broad period thesis
The early-1990s BANK-RURAL landscape is not yet a modern rural-commercial-bank system. The dominant institutional memory is a dispersed rural credit cooperative network embedded in county/township local economies, with nominal cooperative identity but strong administrative and local-government influence, important managerial and functional ties to Agricultural Bank of China, extensive village-level service/agency networks, regulated interest rates and planned-credit legacies, and asset quality heavily shaped by township-and-village enterprises, collective-sector borrowers, agriculture and local policy priorities.

The period also contains important adjacent channels that must not be collapsed into RCCs: Agricultural Bank policy/commercial functions, postal savings as a rural deposit collector, informal private credit, supply-and-marketing/cooperative-sector finance, and rural cooperative foundations or similar local quasi-financial vehicles. Several of these later become reform, cleanup or boundary problems.

## 1. Institutional landscape and lineage

### Lead 1 — 农村信用合作社 is the core institutional label
Rural credit cooperatives are the primary BANK-RURAL actor for this slice. Modern labels such as rural commercial bank or village bank should not be projected backward. Later institutions can inherit RCC lineages without making their earlier balance sheets comparable to modern entities.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 2 — 乡镇信用社 / 基层信用社 as operating units
Grassroots rural credit cooperatives likely operated at township or similar local levels, close to depositors and borrowers. The economic model relied on dense local information and physical presence but also created governance fragmentation and uneven risk discipline.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 3 — 县联社 / county union as coordination layer
County-level rural credit cooperative unions are a key organizational/search term. Their exact legal and managerial authority needs Evidence later, but they plausibly coordinated settlement, liquidity, personnel, supervision or pooled services across local cooperatives.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 4 — 信用站 / 代办站 / village-level service points
Village credit stations or agency points are an important long-tail network concept. They could extend deposit collection and customer reach beyond full branches while introducing cash-control, agency, fraud, data-quality and accountability risks.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 5 — cooperative ownership was not equivalent to modern member governance
The word 'cooperative' should not be interpreted as strong member control or modern mutual-bank governance. Member/shareholder rights, local cadre influence, higher-level administrative control and managerial autonomy could diverge sharply.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 6 — ABC management relationship is structurally important
Before the later formal separation of RCCs from Agricultural Bank of China, RCCs are strongly recalled as being under an important ABC managerial/administrative relationship. Exact legal wording and dates must be verified later. This creates a crucial boundary: RCCs were distinct institutions/economic units but were not fully autonomous from the agricultural state-bank apparatus.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 7 — RCC versus ABC functional overlap
Both ABC and RCC networks could serve rural borrowers and depositors, but their customer scale, policy duties, institutional hierarchy and local embeddedness differed. The early-1990s rural finance system should not be modeled as a clean one-bank/one-function allocation.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 8 — modern RCB continuity must be treated as lineage, not identity
Later rural commercial banks can descend from RCC restructuring, but legal entity, ownership, capital, accounting perimeter and governance may change materially. Long-run studies must distinguish economic lineage from legal continuity.

Classification: useful refinement.

## 2. Borrower structure and asset mix

### Lead 9 — township-and-village enterprises are a major risk/profit engine
Township-and-village enterprises (乡镇企业 / 乡镇工业 / TVEs) are likely central to RCC credit demand in many regions. Their expansion gave local lenders growth and information advantages while concentrating risk in local industrial clusters and local-government-linked collective borrowers.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 10 — collective enterprises differ from modern private SMEs
A TVE or collective enterprise can have blurred property rights and local-government backing unlike a later private SME. Credit analysis, guarantees and expected support therefore differ from modern SME lending.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 11 — rural household production credit is not modern retail credit
Household loans were more likely tied to farming, livestock, sideline production, small trade or household enterprise than to mortgages/cards/consumer finance. Calling the book 'retail' would obscure production and seasonal cash-flow risk.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 12 — seasonal agriculture creates maturity and liquidity mismatches
Planting, harvest, procurement and livestock cycles create seasonal credit needs. Deposit inflows and loan demand may not align, making local liquidity management and higher-level funding channels economically important.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 13 — collateral scarcity raises the importance of local information
Formal collateral markets and registries were less developed, especially for household and collective borrowers. Relationship knowledge, local reputation, guarantors and administrative connections likely substituted for hard collateral and standardized scoring.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 14 — guarantees can socialize local credit risk
Mutual guarantees, collective guarantees, local enterprise guarantees or administrative support could reduce apparent borrower risk ex ante while creating correlated losses when a local industry or township economy weakens.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 15 — agriculture policy objectives can blur commercial credit tests
Some lending may reflect policy encouragement or local development objectives even when the RCC is notionally cooperative/commercial. The policy-versus-commercial boundary is therefore porous.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 16 — poverty/alleviation credit is a boundary lead, not assumed RCC core
Subsidized or targeted rural-poverty credit existed in the broader system, but exact institutional routing among ABC, policy programs, RCCs and local fiscal channels needs Evidence. Do not automatically assign every rural policy loan to RCCs.

Classification: useful refinement.

## 3. Liability model and deposit mobilization

### Lead 17 — household savings are the fundamental funding franchise
RCCs likely relied heavily on rural household and local-enterprise deposits. Dense local networks created a franchise advantage but also tied funding stability to local trust and service quality.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 18 — deposit gathering is a core economic function even where loan autonomy is constrained
An RCC can be valuable to the system as a deposit mobilizer regardless of how freely it can allocate credit. Deposit collection and local credit allocation should be analyzed separately.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 19 — postal savings can drain rural deposits from local credit intermediation
Postal savings is an important adjacent mechanism: rural post-office savings collection could attract household deposits without recycling them locally through RCC lending in the same way. Contemporary rural-finance debates may describe this as a 'siphoning' or funds-outflow problem. Exact institutional settlement arrangements require Evidence.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 20 — deposit competition can involve trust, convenience and administrative reach rather than market pricing alone
With controlled interest rates, competitive advantage may come from proximity, cash access, reputation, service, workplace/local-government links and convenience. Any high-interest or bonus-pricing practices should be verified rather than assumed.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 21 — local deposit concentration can amplify local shocks
Where deposits and loans come from the same county/industry cluster, a negative local shock can hit both borrower quality and depositor confidence/business cash balances.

Classification: new category.

## 4. Liquidity, settlement and hierarchy

### Lead 22 — grassroots cooperatives need a liquidity/settlement hierarchy
Small local institutions cannot efficiently operate as isolated balance sheets. County unions, ABC relationships, central-bank facilities or inter-cooperative transfers likely mattered for settlement and liquidity. Exact mechanisms and eligibility must be verified.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 23 — seasonal liquidity is distinct from solvency
A cooperative can be economically solvent yet need seasonal liquidity during crop/enterprise funding peaks. Conversely, repeated liquidity support can mask asset-quality weakness. Later analysis must separate these states.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 24 — administrative credit controls can bind rural lenders unevenly
National credit tightening or quota control may transmit through higher-level institutions and affect RCC lending availability even when local deposit growth is strong.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 25 — 1993-era macro tightening is a major stress lead
The early-1990s overheating/inflation episode and subsequent credit tightening likely exposed weak TVEs, local investment projects and payment chains. For rural lenders, the mechanism may be slower borrower cash conversion, arrears, guarantee-chain stress and tighter incremental credit.

Classification: new category, high importance.

## 5. Governance and control

### Lead 26 — local-government influence can dominate nominal cooperative governance
Township/county officials may influence borrower selection, enterprise rescue, personnel or credit priorities. This creates a soft-budget-constraint mechanism analogous to but institutionally distinct from city-bank local-state influence.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 27 — ABC administrative influence and local-government influence can coexist
RCCs may face dual or multiple principals: agricultural-bank hierarchy, local government/cadres, cooperative members and regulators. This can create unclear accountability when loans sour.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 28 — weak property rights in collective enterprises complicate related-party concepts
Credit to locally connected collective entities may be economically related or politically directed without fitting later formal related-party definitions.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 29 — personnel rotation and cadre-bank ties are a possible governance channel
Managers may be selected or influenced through administrative systems rather than purely professional bank labor markets. Exact personnel structures need verification.

Classification: useful refinement.

### Lead 30 — insider/fraud risk rises with dispersed cash operations
Village-level cash collection, manual records, local familiarity and weak independent audit can create embezzlement, fictitious loans, unauthorized guarantees or deposit misappropriation risks. This is a long-tail operational-risk lead.

Classification: new category.

## 6. Asset-quality vocabulary and metric semantics

### Lead 31 — 一逾两呆 is a critical old vocabulary/search key
Older Chinese bank asset-quality discussions used categories recalled as overdue, sluggish/doubtful and bad/dead loans ('一逾两呆' / 逾期、呆滞、呆账). Exact definitions and thresholds require Evidence. These categories are not directly comparable to later five-category NPL standards.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 32 — reported bad-loan ratios are definition-dependent
A 1990s 'bad loan' measure may omit overdue or restructured exposures that later frameworks would treat differently. Cross-time NPL comparisons without semantic normalization are unsafe.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 33 — rolled-over loans can delay loss recognition
Local lenders may extend or renew credit to distressed TVEs/agricultural borrowers to avoid immediate default, preserve employment or maintain local production. Renewal can be economically rational or politically pressured; reported delinquency can lag underlying weakness.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 34 — collective guarantees can hide correlation until stress
Guarantees from local enterprises or collective bodies may appear to improve credit quality but fail jointly during regional downturns.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 35 — write-offs and fiscal/owner support may not follow modern provisioning rules
Historical loss absorption could involve administrative transfers, capital support, special cleanup or implicit forbearance rather than standardized expected-loss provisioning.

Classification: useful refinement.

## 7. Parallel and adjacent rural-finance channels

### Lead 36 — 农村合作基金会 is an important parallel/dead-end reform lead
Rural cooperative foundations or similarly named local funds are strongly recalled as expanding in parts of rural China before later cleanup. They were not simply ordinary licensed RCCs. Their deposit-like fundraising and lending/investment activities can create boundary confusion and local credit competition.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 37 — rural cooperative foundations can reflect unmet local credit demand
Where formal RCC/ABC credit was constrained, local quasi-financial foundations could attract funds and finance township enterprises or local projects. This mechanism is important even if the institutions are ultimately classified outside the BANK-RURAL bank perimeter.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 38 — cooperative-foundation distress is a later warning embedded in this slice
Aggressive local lending, weak supervision, quasi-deposit promises and political backing can create latent losses before formal cleanup. Exact failure timing belongs to later slices/Evidence, but the institutional antecedent matters here.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 39 — informal private lending is a persistent outside option
Households and rural entrepreneurs can use family loans, rotating associations, private lenders or trade credit when bank credit is rationed. Formal loan growth therefore understates total rural credit.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 40 — Wenzhou/private-finance type regions are counterexamples to RCC dominance
Some entrepreneurial regions may rely much more heavily on private/informal finance and commercial-bank links than on a standardized cooperative channel. This is a regional archetype rather than a new bank type.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 41 — supply-and-marketing cooperatives are an adjacent institutional network
供销社 and related cooperative-commercial networks can intersect with rural cash flow, procurement, trade and local credit relationships. They should not be automatically classified as banks, but veteran rural-finance sources may mention them alongside RCCs.

Classification: useful refinement.

## 8. Policy/commercial boundary and 1994 transition leads

### Lead 42 — ABC still carries policy/commercial overlap in the early slice
Before policy-bank separation is fully established, agricultural state-bank operations can mix policy objectives and commercial balance-sheet functions. RCC interaction with ABC therefore cannot be understood using only later commercial-bank categories.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 43 — Agricultural Development Bank creation is a boundary shift lead
The emergence of Agricultural Development Bank of China around the end of this slice is a major policy-versus-commercial boundary change. Exact transfer of grain/policy-lending functions, accounts and personnel requires Evidence, but the transition can affect ABC/RCC role expectations.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 44 — grain procurement finance should not be blindly assigned to RCCs
Policy grain/cotton/oil procurement financing belongs to a specialized policy-credit story around ABC/ADBC and state purchasing systems. RCCs may interact locally but should not absorb the whole mechanism into their core franchise.

Classification: useful refinement.

### Lead 45 — 1994 fiscal reform can alter local-government incentives without an instantaneous banking break
Tax-sharing reform changes central-local fiscal relations, but rural-bank balance sheets likely adjust gradually. Avoid assigning a single-year mechanical breakpoint to local credit behavior without Evidence.

Classification: useful refinement.

## 9. TVE boom, overheating and credit-cycle mechanisms

### Lead 46 — TVE credit growth can be self-reinforcing
Local enterprise expansion raises employment, deposits, fiscal revenue and collateral values, encouraging more RCC lending. The same local multiplier can reverse during tightening.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 47 — 三角债 / payment arrears can transmit into rural-bank asset quality
Enterprise receivables/payables chains and delayed payments can convert liquidity stress into overdue bank credit. This is especially relevant where many borrowers belong to one local industrial cluster.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 48 — local-government guarantees can preserve credit after cash flow weakens
Explicit or implicit local support may encourage rollover of weak TVEs, delaying recognition and deepening eventual losses.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 49 — inflation can raise nominal working-capital needs
High inflation and commodity/input-price changes can increase nominal credit demand even without equivalent real output growth, making nominal loan-growth comparisons misleading.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 50 — real deposit behavior under inflation deserves separate attention
Households may shift between cash, bank deposits, goods or informal investments as inflation expectations change. Deposit-rate regulation means real returns matter to rural deposit mobilization.

Classification: new category.

## 10. Regional archetypes

### Lead 51 — coastal TVE-rich regions
Jiangsu/Zhejiang/Guangdong-like areas may combine strong rural industrialization, dense enterprise networks and faster deposit growth, giving RCCs more corporate-like working-capital exposure than farm-dominant regions.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 52 — grain/agriculture-dominant interior regions
Loan demand may be more seasonal, policy-linked and household/agriculture oriented; income volatility can depend more on harvests and procurement conditions.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 53 — poor western/remote counties
Thin deposits, high operating costs, sparse borrowers and policy/social-service expectations can make pure commercial sustainability harder. Physical network economics are structurally different from affluent coastal counties.

### Lead 54 — peri-urban counties blur rural/urban customer labels
Fast-growing county economies near major cities can host industrial borrowers and property/commerce growth while remaining under RCC institutions. Geography and institutional lineage must be separated.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 55 — collective-dominant versus private-enterprise regions
Collective ownership may create stronger local-government support expectations; private-enterprise regions may rely more on personal guarantees, informal finance and relationship lending. This affects governance and loss transmission.

## 12. Failed or dead-end mechanisms

### Lead 56 — administrative lending without clear loss owner
When local government, cooperative structures and higher-level bank management all influence loans, nobody may bear full ex ante responsibility for credit risk. This can produce chronic rollover and later cleanup.

Classification: new category, high importance.

### Lead 57 — member-share capital may be too weak to absorb losses
Nominal cooperative equity can be small relative to credit risk, especially if member shares are dispersed or quasi-deposit-like in expectations. Exact capital structures need Evidence.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 58 — fragmented small-cooperative model can sacrifice scale economies
Many small units may struggle with professional risk management, technology, treasury, audit and specialist staff. Later consolidation reforms can be understood partly as response to these scale constraints.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 59 — over-centralization can also destroy local information advantages
Conversely, moving decisions upward can reduce local borrower knowledge and responsiveness. Reform trade-offs should not assume bigger institutions are automatically safer or better at rural lending.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 60 — quasi-fiscal local credit can crowd out household/private borrowers
Credit directed to collective/local-government projects or TVEs can consume limited funding and reduce access for smaller farm households or private entrepreneurs.

Classification: new category.

## 13. Contemporaneous expectations and later falsification leads

### Lead 61 — cooperative identity was expected to align local savings with local credit
A plausible contemporaneous ideal was that member-based local institutions would recycle local deposits to rural households and enterprises. Later governance and capital problems suggest the cooperative label alone did not guarantee this outcome.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 62 — local knowledge was expected to solve information asymmetry
Close borrower relationships can improve screening, but they can also increase political capture, favoritism and tolerance of weak borrowers. Local knowledge is both franchise asset and governance risk.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 63 — fast TVE growth may have been read as structural credit quality
Boom-time local industrial growth can make relationship lending appear unusually safe. Tightening and enterprise restructuring can reveal that prior performance partly reflected macro/regional tailwinds.

Classification: new category.

### Lead 64 — administrative backing may have been mistaken for credit protection
Local-government influence can create an expectation of rescue, but support capacity and willingness are constrained. This soft-budget expectation is a key risk lead.

Classification: new category.

## 14. Metric and accounting caution set
- Do not compare early-1990s 'bad loans' directly with later NPL ratios.
- Separate overdue, sluggish/doubtful and bad/dead loan vocabulary before mapping to later classifications.
- Separate RCC balance sheet from county-union or consolidated-system aggregates.
- Separate nominal loan growth from real loan growth during inflation.
- Separate rural deposits collected locally from funds actually available for local lending.
- Treat member shares / 股金 carefully: economic deposit-like behavior versus true loss-absorbing equity requires Evidence.
- Do not count village credit stations as branches without checking reporting definitions.
- Do not treat later RCB legal entity age as continuous with the oldest RCC predecessor without explicit lineage method.
- Do not classify rural cooperative foundations as licensed RCCs merely because both use 'cooperative' vocabulary.

## 15. Counterexamples

### Counterexample A — strong rural industrial county
A prosperous TVE/private-enterprise county can produce strong deposit growth and profitable relationship lending, showing that rural geography does not imply weak economics.

### Counterexample B — poor agricultural county
A sparse deposit base, high service cost and policy-intensive borrower mix can make the same RCC institutional form financially fragile.

### Counterexample C — informal-finance-dominant entrepreneurial region
Formal RCC credit can be only one component of rural finance where private lending and trade credit are large.

### Counterexample D — administratively supported collective borrower
Apparent repayment strength can depend on local-government coordination rather than standalone enterprise cash flow.

### Counterexample E — high deposits but local credit shortage
Postal savings or other fund-outflow mechanisms can coexist with substantial local household savings while local enterprises still report credit scarcity.
